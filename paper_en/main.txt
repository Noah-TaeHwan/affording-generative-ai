Localised Entry, Global Frontier:
How Generative-AI Subscriptions Are Priced Across
Countries
TaeHwan Oh*
October 2026

Abstract
Generative-AI assistants are sold through one subscription menu in every country while national
incomes differ a hundredfold. Two audits of tax-inclusive prices in every national Apple App Store
storefront, the second covering ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, six other AI assistants, 21 general subscription apps and four apps with embedded AI features, show which tiers are priced locally. Within
the same firm and storefront, where currency, tax and the platform’s conversion cancel, the relative
prices of the $20 standard and $100–500 high-usage AI tiers vary little with income (absolute slopes
at most 0.022), whereas the same firms’ YouTube and X Premium subscriptions are 50–73% cheaper
in the median low- or lower-middle-income storefront (Microsoft 365 Personal 15%). Two capped
AI entry tiers are about 40% cheaper (ChatGPT Go, Character.AI Lite); every localised AI tier is
a capped entry tier, but not every capped tier is localised. Relative to the platform’s automatically
generated price, estimated from 30 small-developer apps, the frontier tiers’ deviations are centred
on zero; Google Play’s lowest ChatGPT price and Microsoft’s web prices closely match the App
Store. In a four-option versioning model with an income-invariant inference cost, an unconstrained
vendor would pass an entry-tier discount on to the frontier tier; instead, the frontier-to-entry dollar
gap widens where incomes are low, which rejects that benchmark but cannot separate a held frontier
price from frontier valuations that do not scale with income. A $20 plan costs 0.65% of per-capita
income in the median high-income economy and 29% in the median low-income one.
Keywords: generative AI; international price discrimination; versioning; platform pricing; affordability; digital divide.
JEL codes: L11; L15; L81; L86; D42; O33.

Introduction

Some digital goods are priced to market: online retailers charge less where incomes are lower, with
cross-country price-income elasticities of about 0.14–0.18 for tradable goods (Simonovska, 2015),
streaming services set country-specific prices (Colbjørnsen et al., 2022), and Spotify’s country prices
raise its revenue over a single world price while transferring surplus to lower-income countries (Waldfogel, 2020). Many digital subscriptions are not: in the App Store benchmark of this paper only 8
of 21 general subscription apps localise any plan (Appendix Table E.8). The informative comparison is therefore within the firm: generative-AI firms localise some of their products and not others.
OpenAI, Anthropic and Google sell the same $20 standard tier and the same $100–500 high-usage
tiers in every country, and the only AI products whose prices are lower where incomes are lower are
capability-capped entry tiers. Which tiers of the menu do vendors price for local markets, and what
keeps the others at the U.S.-anchored price? This paper documents the pattern with two audits of every
national Apple App Store storefront, compares each firm’s AI tiers with the other subscriptions it sells
through the same storefronts, estimates how much of the remaining variation is the platform’s own
tax-and-currency conversion rather than vendor pricing, and asks whether a simple versioning model
with a positive, dollar-denominated marginal cost of inference and cross-border arbitrage can account
for where localisation appears and where it does not.
The question matters for two reasons. For industrial organization, AI subscriptions are versioned
products with a positive marginal cost of serving each subscriber: unlike a copy of a song or a film, every
conversation with a frontier model consumes computation that is priced in dollars worldwide. Versioning
with positive marginal or usage costs has been studied in a single market (Jones and Mendelson, 2011;
Chellappa and Mehra, 2018); what the audit adds is the multi-market margin, where the same dollar cost
meets a hundredfold spread of incomes, a platform that converts prices automatically, and the possibility
of buying across borders. For policy, the pattern determines who can afford frontier capability. A
uniform dollar price for the standard tier costs 0.65% of per-capita income a year in the median highincome economy and 29% in the median low-income one, and the entry tiers that vendors do localise
withhold the frontier model.
The evidence comes from four sources collected for this paper. The first is a two-wave audit of
the tax-inclusive local prices that Apple’s App Store listed on 27 September and 16 October 2026 for
every plan of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini in every national storefront (218 codes, of which 170–
173 return local price lists). The second wave extends the audit to six other AI assistants (Perplexity,
Grok, Mistral, Poe, Character.AI and Microsoft 365 Copilot), 21 general subscription apps (among them
YouTube, YouTube Music, Google One, Duolingo, Telegram, Discord, Snapchat, LinkedIn, Dropbox,
X and NordVPN) and four apps that sell AI features inside a general product (Canva, CapCut, Notion,
Grammarly): 34 apps, 7,412 app-storefront queries and 30,817 item-storefront prices, with the fingerprint of every page recorded in the package and the pages themselves deposited with the replication
package. The third is a panel of 30 small-developer subscription apps in the same storefronts, used to
estimate the price that Apple generates automatically from a U.S. base price; the estimate is exact only
at price points that small developers use, such as $19.99, and is extrapolated with a storefront multiplier
elsewhere. The fourth is a cross-channel check of Google Play’s lowest in-app price for twelve apps in
30 countries and of Microsoft’s web store in 57 countries.
Three findings follow. First, within the same firm and the same storefront, where currency, taxes

Section 3 develops a model under which a profit-maximising vendor would price this way, and
states which of its implications the cross-section can and cannot test. A multi-market monopolist sells a
free tier, a capped entry tier and a frontier tier to consumers whose valuations scale with local income.
Serving a frontier subscriber costs cH a month in inference, a cost that is denominated in dollars and does
not fall with local income; serving a capped entry subscriber costs much less. With lognormal valuations,
the profit-maximising price of each tier’s increment over the tier below has an income elasticity that
falls with the marginal-cost share of that increment, from one when the marginal cost is zero towards
zero when the margin is thin (Proposition A.2). Because the frontier price is the entry price plus that
increment, an unconstrained vendor would pass any entry-tier discount on to the frontier tier, so a frontier
price that stays put while the entry tier is localised is the signature of a binding constraint within the
nested menu (Proposition A.3); frontier valuations that do not scale with income would produce the
same pattern (Remark 1). The platform’s default schedule is the zero-effort price, and deviating from it
carries a small fixed cost, so vendors deviate only where the gain is large: for the entry tier in poorer
markets, and not for the frontier tier, where inference cost leaves little profit at stake in re-pricing.
Cross-border arbitrage caps the dollar gap that any tier can open between storefronts; a gap that is a
large discount on an $8 capped tier is a small one on a $200 tier, so arbitrage can help hold the highusage tiers at the default but not, on its own, the $20 tiers (Proposition A.4). The model yields five
predictions, P1–P5, of which Section 5 tests P1–P3 against listed prices and treats P4 as a consistency
check, with P5 discussed in Section 7; its demand side implies that the margin on which affordability
policy would bite is paid access to frontier capability.

subscription prices to the literature on international price discrimination and pricing to market (Krugman, 1986; Goldberg and Knetter, 1997; Atkeson and Burstein, 2008; Cavallo et al., 2014), on uniform
pricing by multi-market and multi-product firms (DellaVigna and Gentzkow, 2019; Shiller and Waldfogel, 2011) and on the decomposition of cross-border price differences into taxes, costs and markups
(Goldberg and Verboven, 2001; Gopinath et al., 2011); the within-firm, within-storefront design and the

the margin that vendors’ own pricing sets.

2.2

How App Store prices are set

The prices this paper audits are listed by Apple’s App Store, and four platform rules shape them. First,
a developer chooses a base storefront and a price there on a grid of price points; App Store Connect
then “provides comparable prices for all 175 App Store countries and regions, taking into account taxes
and foreign exchange rates” and rounding to local price conventions, and a developer may override the
generated price in any storefront (Apple, 2026b,a). The listed prices include value-added or goods-andservices taxes in the storefronts where Apple collects them (Section 4). The automatically generated
price is the default schedule referred to below; the AI vendors’ U.S. prices sit on the price-point grid
($19.99, $199.99), consistent with a U.S. base storefront. Second, for auto-renewable subscriptions Apple does not refresh the generated prices after later tax or exchange-rate changes: each of its periodic
price updates since February 2025 states that the update “excludes auto-renewable subscriptions” (Apple, 2026c). A subscription’s default is therefore the comparable price of the date on which its price was
set or last reset, which makes the schedule estimated in Section 5.3 a mixture of vintages; Section 5.3
tests the implications. Third, Apple decides which storefronts exist and in which currency each is denominated: 45 of the 218 two-letter codes audited have no storefront of their own and redirect to the U.S.
store, and 102 of the 164 storefronts in the first-wave price regressions, including every storefront in a
low-income economy, are denominated in U.S. dollars; in 16 of the U.S.-dollar storefronts the generated
price nonetheless embeds a tax of more than 5%. Fourth, Apple retains an ad valorem commission on
subscription revenue, 15 or 30% depending on the developer’s size and the subscription’s tenure but,
under Apple’s standard terms, the same share in every storefront for a given plan, so the commission
scales all of a plan’s prices alike (Wang and Wright, 2017); with a positive marginal cost it raises the
cost share of the price, which the model of Section 3 absorbs into the cost. Because the default schedule
embeds taxes, which rise with income across storefronts, a plan left at the default will show a small
positive income slope in dollars even though the vendor has done nothing; Section 5.3 measures that
slope from the small-developer panel and treats a plan’s deviation from the default as the vendor’s active
pricing decision, and Section 5.1 shows what can be learned without the schedule at all.

3.2

Supply: inference costs, the platform default and arbitrage

Three features of the supply side distinguish AI subscriptions from the information goods of the versioning literature.
Positive, income-invariant marginal cost. Serving a subscriber on tier k for a month costs ck ≥ 0 in
computation, with cH > cL ≥ cF and a cost per unit of service that rises from the entry to the frontier
increment, (cL − cF )/(qL − qF ) < (cH − cL )/(qH − qL ), which keeps the vendor’s optimal menu nested.
The cost is incurred in dollars at the vendor’s data centres, so it does not fall with the subscriber’s income,
and for the frontier tier it is a substantial share of the price: heavy users of a $20 plan can consume more
computation than the plan collects, which is why vendors cap usage and sell higher allowances at higher
prices.

the capped product is worth v(qH − qL ) less to her than the frontier tier she buys at home. Arbitrage
therefore caps the dollar gap between two storefronts by the same amount for every tier, and a gap that
is a large proportional discount on a cheap capped tier is a small one on the frontier tiers.

(Appendix E). In the 16 dollar-priced storefronts whose schedule embeds a tax of more than 5%, Google
lists AI Pro at exactly $19.99 in 15 and AI Ultra at $199.99 in all 8 in which it is offered, Microsoft lists

and OpenAI lists Plus at $19.99 in five. A vendor that overrides the default there and still chooses the
U.S. dollar price is not held by the cost of overriding; these listings fit a deliberate uniform dollar price,
as in the segmented-demand case of Remark 1, better than the override-cost mechanism. The robust fact
is narrower than “frontier tiers sit on the schedule”, and it is the fact that matters for the model: frontier
prices are not lower where incomes are lower relative to the platform’s conversion, even though vendors
actively set storefront-specific prices for them.

5.4

The within-storefront ratio and the dollar increment

Because both plans are listed in the same storefront under the same currency and tax rule, the ratio of the
standard price to the entry price is free of the conversion that affects dollar prices. Prediction P3 holds
for the ratio: ChatGPT Plus costs 2.5 times ChatGPT Go in the United States, 2.9 times in the median
high-income storefront, 3.3 in the median upper-middle-, 3.8 in the median lower-middle- and 4.0 in the
median low-income storefront, identically in both waves (Figure 4; 167 storefronts in the second wave).
The log Go-to-Plus ratio has an income slope of 0.104 (currency-clustered s.e. 0.007; 164 economies;
equivalently −0.104 for the Plus-to-Go ratio), which on a common sample is exactly the difference
between the Go and Plus slopes; it is 0.113 within the 102 USD-priced storefronts, 0.112 without them
and 0.125 without USD and EUR storefronts (Appendix Table E.3), so the entry-tier gradient is not an
artefact of currency conversion.
The ratio rises as income falls under every regime of the model, so the discriminating quantity is
the dollar increment between the two plans. Table 7 measures it two ways. Relative to the increment
between the two plans’ schedule prices, the Plus-to-Go dollar gap is 4% wider than the schedule gap

in the median high-income storefront, 17% wider in the median upper-middle-income storefront and
25% wider in the median lower-middle- and low-income storefronts, with an income slope of −0.047

the unconstrained optimum the frontier price follows the entry price down and the increment narrows
(Proposition A.3). The data reject the unconstrained optimum of the nested menu. They are consistent
with a frontier price held near the default and equally with the segmented-demand case of Remark 1,
which listed prices cannot separate.

5.5

Affordability

With frontier prices at the U.S.-anchored level everywhere, burdens are set by income. Table 8 consolidates the affordability measures by income group, and Figure 5 shows the $20 plan across the income distribution. At a uniform $20 the median burden is 0.65% of per-capita income in high-income
economies, 3.09% in upper-middle-, 8.73% in lower-middle- and 28.9% in low-income economies, a
45-fold difference between the first and last groups; at the tax-inclusive local price of ChatGPT Plus in
the economies with a listing the medians are 0.75%, 3.27%, 8.89% and 28.9%. The $20 tier meets the
2% benchmark in every high-income economy, in 22% of upper-middle-income economies and in no
lower-middle- or low-income economy, and the 113 of 201 economies above it are home to 60% of the
covered working-age population. Indexed to local price levels the burdens would fall to 0.40%, 1.20%,
3.38% and 10.7%, still five times the benchmark in the median low-income economy. For the poorest
fifth of households a $20 plan costs a median 1.6% of mean income in high-income economies but 33%
in lower-middle- and 73% in low-income economies, and it fails the benchmark even for the richest fifth
in every low-income economy with survey data and in 98% of lower-middle-income economies. Only
the entry tiers fall below 3% in the median lower-middle-income economy: ChatGPT Go, the localised
tier, at 2.6%, and Google AI Plus, which is not localised but has a $4.99 U.S. price, at 2.1% (Appendix
Table E.4); in the median low-income economy both cost 7.2%. Bringing the $20 tier to the benchmark
under uniform pricing would require a subsidy of 35%, 77% and 93% of the price in the three poorer
groups ($84, $185 and $223 per subscriber a year), or 0.08%, 0.43% and 1.55% of GNI if one tenth
of the working-age population were covered; applying the entry tier’s U.S.-dollar income slope (0.143,
which includes the schedule’s 0.033) to the $20 tier would lower the median low-income burden only
to 14.8%, and applying only its deviation from the schedule (0.110) to 17.3% (Appendix Table E.7).
These are accounting magnitudes, not estimates of behaviour, and the 2% benchmark is a broadband
convention rather than a threshold derived for AI subscriptions.

6

The public usage indicators: what they can and cannot show

The model’s demand side implies that, with a free tier in every market and paid tiers priced above the
free hurdle, whether people use AI at all depends on connectivity, skills and language rather than on the
price of paid tiers, whereas paying for more capability depends on the ratio of price to income (Proposition A.1). No public indicator observes paid adoption, so the two usage indicators cannot test that
implication; Appendix D reports what they show. Any use is moderately income-graded: across the
economies with country-specific Microsoft estimates, the elasticity of the AI user share with respect to
GNI per capita is 0.370 (s.e. 0.021), unchanged by controls for internet use, education, urbanisation,
age structure, digital payments and region, and median user shares in the second quarter of 2026 were
32.3%, 18.8%, 12.0% and 7.6% from the high- to the low-income group. Per-capita activity on one
frontier assistant is twice as income-graded: on the 96 economies covered by both indicators, the elasticity of the Anthropic AI Usage Index is 0.711 (s.e. 0.036) against 0.371 (s.e. 0.026) for the Microsoft
user share, a difference of 0.340 (clustered s.e. 0.034) that survives the robustness checks of Appendix
Table D.4. What the contrast identifies is limited by the measurement of the two indicators. The index
pools free and paid accounts: its gradient is the sum of the gradients of the share using any generative-AI
product (which the Microsoft indicator measures), of Claude’s share among AI users and of conversations per Claude user, and the last of these is an attenuated version of the paid-margin gradient that
Proposition A.1 concerns (Appendix A); Anthropic’s publication floor censors small low- and lowermiddle-income economies, so the common sample contains one low-income economy; Microsoft’s estimator shrinks noisy countries toward the global average; and Claude’s share among AI users varies with
language support, brand reach and the occupational composition of work (Daepp and Slaughter, 2026;
Gmyrek et al., 2025). The contrast is therefore consistent with the model’s ordering of margins, which
the $20 tier’s slope satisfies unless more than about nine in ten users pay (Appendix A), and equally with
these measurement and composition explanations; testing paid upgrading would require country-level
user counts by subscription status and effective prices. The staggered rollout of ChatGPT Go offers
price variation unrelated to income, but its comparison group was partly treated within days, the treated
economies lie in one region and telecom bundles in India confound the largest case, so Appendix F

21

reports it as a descriptive episode without causal interpretation.

7

Discussion

7.1

Interpretation

The price evidence is consistent with the model’s predictions: frontier tiers on or near the platform
schedule, with deviations centred on zero (P1); localised AI tiers that are all capped entry tiers, although not every capped tier is localised (P2); a within-storefront dollar gap between the frontier and
the entry tier that widens as income falls (P3), the one implication that separates a held frontier price
from an unconstrained one; and within-firm contrasts in which the same firm localises its products without an AI allowance and not its frontier AI tiers (P4). What the increment test establishes is narrower
than the model’s mechanism: it rejects the unconstrained optimum of the nested menu, and it does
not attribute the hold to inference cost. The model’s account is that an unconstrained vendor would
pass the entry tier’s discount on to the frontier tier, that with a high inference-cost share little profit is
at stake in doing so, and that, where vendors have not overridden it, the platform’s default holds the
frontier price, which is why the dollar gap between Plus and Go widens in poorer storefronts (Proposition A.3). Where vendors have overridden the default (Appendix Table E.13), they chose the U.S.
dollar price, which points to segmented frontier demand or a deliberate uniform dollar price rather than
to the override cost. Four explanations therefore remain side by side, and the evidence speaks to each
differently. A high inference-cost share together with an override cost fits the held frontier price and
the localised entry tiers, but not the overrides to $19.99 in taxed dollar storefronts. Cross-border arbitrage caps the dollar gap that any tier can open; because ChatGPT Go is sustained $3–4 below its U.S.
price, a friction common to all plans could help hold the $100–500 tiers within a few per cent of the
default but not the $20 tiers (Proposition A.4), and because tax-inclusive gaps of tens of dollars persist
between rich storefronts (Appendix A.4, footnote), frictions are probably large enough that arbitrage
binds on no tier. A uniform-dollar pricing policy, or anchoring on the U.S. price point, fits every frontier
listing, including the overrides, but says nothing about why the entry tiers are localised. Segmented demand among frontier buyers, whose valuations do not scale with local income (Remark 1), produces the
same signs as the held frontier price, including the widening dollar gap, and fits the overrides as well.
The cross-section cannot separate these. Other explanations cover parts of the pattern. Inattention to
storefront-specific pricing for new products does not: the same vendors localised YouTube, X Premium
and, moderately, Microsoft 365 Personal through the same storefronts, and OpenAI localised ChatGPT
Go within weeks of its launch. Market-entry sequencing does not either: Go’s deviation from the schedule rises smoothly with income across the 161 storefronts rather than marking the launch markets in
India and Asia. Product-level demand may matter: YouTube and X Premium face local competitors
and local content, and a productivity subscription may have a clientele whose valuations scale less with
local income than a consumer subscription’s; the firm-by-storefront fixed effects absorb a firm’s pricing
capacity, not these differences, and the comparison across firms does not settle them (X Premium pays
no content royalties and is localised as heavily as YouTube; Microsoft 365 Personal, a productivity product, only moderately). The closest contrast in the data is within Google, between YouTube Premium, at
$20.99 in the United States, and Google AI Pro, at $19.99, sold by the same firm through the same storefronts under the same App Store account rules: the first is 57% cheaper than the second in the median

22

lower-income storefront (Table 3), so neither the firm, the price level nor the platform’s rules account for
the difference, and what differs is the product. Measuring the arbitrage friction beyond the lower bound
that the entry tier reveals would require observing a change in frictions, such as the introduction of local
identity or payment checks, with prices held fixed.
Prediction P5 concerns how vendors make frontier capability cheaper where they do. The localised
AI tiers in the audit are all capped or degraded versions: ChatGPT Go withholds the frontier reasoning
model and Character.AI Lite caps usage and features; Google AI Plus and SuperGrok Lite are capped
tiers that are not localised, and Microsoft 365 Personal carries a small Copilot allowance. No vendor
sells the frontier model at a localised price with a usage cap, although the allowance structure of the
upper tiers shows that caps are available to them. In the model, service is one-dimensional, so a strict
cap on the frontier model and a weaker model are the same instrument: either lowers the service and the
inference cost of the cheap tier and its value to arbitrageurs who would otherwise buy the frontier tier.
Why vendors weaken the model rather than cap it lies outside the model; one candidate is that a capped
frontier tier would be an identical product for light users with high-value tasks, who could trade down to
it at home or buy it abroad, whereas a weaker model is not. The model gives a reason why the frontier
tier stays where it is, and what relaxing that would require: either a cap tight enough to make the local
price cover inference cost, or a third party that pays the difference.

7.2

Policy

Three implications follow for the debate on access to generative AI in lower-income countries. First, the
margin that pricing policy can move is paid access to frontier capability. Cheap tiers already exist in most
poorer markets at $5–8 a month, and the burden of ChatGPT Go is below 3% of per-capita income in the
median lower-middle-income economy, as is that of Google AI Plus, whose cheapness there comes from
a low U.S. price point rather than from localisation; the $20 frontier tier costs 29% of per-capita income
in the median low-income economy and more than 13% even at the synthetic mean resources of its
richest fifth. Second, vendors’ own localisation is unlikely to close that gap: applying OpenAI’s entrytier localisation to the $20 tier would lower the median low-income burden to 14.8% (17.3% if only
the vendor’s deviation from the schedule is applied), still seven to nine times the benchmark, and the
model gives a reason why vendors may not go further. Institutional purchase and subsidies, which pay
the inference cost that a local price cannot cover, are the instruments the magnitudes point to: bringing
the $20 tier to the benchmark costs $84–223 per subscriber a year in the three poorer income groups,
or 0.08–1.55% of GNI for a tenth of the working-age population. Schools, universities, public agencies
and employers can decouple access from household income, as a supervised tutoring programme in
Nigeria did (De Simone et al., 2025), and most evidence on AI’s productivity effects comes from such
employer-provided tools (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2024). Third, quantity rationing could
reconcile frontier access with the cost constraint: a frontier model at a localised price with a strict usage
cap bounds the vendor’s cost per subscriber and deters arbitrage by heavy users, whereas a degraded tier
bounds cost by withholding the model. Third-degree price discrimination tends to raise welfare when
it opens markets that uniform pricing would leave unserved (Varian, 1985; Aguirre et al., 2010), the
relevant case here. Connectivity remains a precondition for any use, and, as an accounting matter, about
half of the use gradient coincides with the connectivity gradient (Appendix D) (World Bank, 2026c;
International Telecommunication Union, 2025a; Hjort and Poulsen, 2019).

7.3

Limitations

The audit observes listed prices in one distribution channel on two days 19 days apart, with a crosschannel check of Google Play’s lowest in-app price and of Microsoft’s web store in a subset of countries;
web checkout prices of the other vendors, carrier billing and promotional bundles are not observed, and
the first wave stored parsed listings rather than pages. The estimated platform schedule comes from
small-developer apps whose own pricing may deviate in some storefronts; it is exact only at the price
points those apps use, is extrapolated with a storefront multiplier at the AI vendors’ $8, $20 and $100–
500 price points, and is weak in six lower-income storefronts, which the validation bounds but does not
eliminate and the within-firm comparisons avoid. The model is a single-vendor versioning model with
one-dimensional service that omits competition, employer access and dynamic pricing; the cross-section
bounds the role of arbitrage but does not separate the cost share of the frontier tier from the income
scaling of its buyers’ valuations. The usage indicators are modelled or sampled estimates that censor
small low-income economies and pool free and paid accounts, their income gradients are descriptive,
and burdens are normalised prices rather than expenditures.

8

Conclusion

Generative-AI assistants are priced as a global product with a local entry ramp. Two audits of every
national App Store storefront show that the $20 standard tiers and the $100–500 high-usage tiers of
OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Perplexity, xAI and Microsoft sit on or near the price that the platform
generates automatically from the U.S. price, with deviations centred on zero and no income-graded
localisation (slopes between −0.016 and 0.004), and that within the same firm and storefront their
prices relative to one another vary little with income. The same firms localise their video, music, socialnetwork and office subscriptions through the same storefronts, YouTube and X heavily and Microsoft
365 moderately, which is consistent with uniformity being a property of products that are costly to serve,
among them the frontier AI tiers, rather than of the firms. The only AI tiers whose prices are lower where
incomes are lower are capped entry tiers, and not all capped tiers are localised: ChatGPT Go is 38%
below the schedule in the median poorer storefront, so that ChatGPT Plus costs two and a half times
Go in the United States and four times in the median low-income storefront, while Google AI Plus and
SuperGrok Lite stay at the schedule.
A versioning model in which inference cost leaves little profit at stake in re-pricing the frontier tier,
cross-border arbitrage caps the dollar gap any tier can open, and a capped tier is cheap to serve and worth
localising, is consistent with where localisation appears and where it does not, and its one implication
that an unconstrained optimum reverses, a frontier-to-entry dollar gap that widens where incomes are
low, holds in the data; the same sign also follows from frontier valuations that do not scale with local
income, which listed prices cannot rule out. Its implication for policy is that the affordability gap in
frontier capability, 0.65% of per-capita income in the median high-income economy against 29% in the
median low-income one, is unlikely to be closed by vendors’ own localisation if the frontier price is
kept uniform by a cost that does not fall with local income, as the model suggests; it can be narrowed
by institutions that pay the inference cost on users’ behalf, or by frontier tiers whose usage caps let a
local price cover that cost. Measuring which of these works requires what no vendor yet publishes: paid
subscriptions, effective prices and entitlements by country.

A.4

The platform default, arbitrage and the binding constraint

Proposition A.3(iv) shows that a localised entry tier combined with a frontier tier at the platform default
is not the vendor’s unconstrained optimum in the maintained model: something must hold the frontier
price up. The model contains two candidates.
The override cost. The listed price is the default unless the vendor overrides it, at a fixed cost K per
plan and storefront. Let GL,c = max pL Πc (pL , PH ) − Πc (PL , PH ) be the gain from localising the entry tier
with the frontier tier left at the default, and GH,c = max pL ,pH Πc (pL , pH ) − max pL Πc (pL , PH ) the further
gain from also re-pricing the frontier tier.
Arbitrage. A consumer whose home storefront is c′ can buy any plan in storefront c at a friction χ,
the cost of holding an account and a payment method in that foreign storefront; χ may differ across pairs
of storefronts but not across the plans bought. Prices in this paragraph and in Proposition A.4(ii)–(iii)
are the tax-inclusive dollar prices that she compares.
Proposition A.4 (Override and arbitrage). (i) GL,c and GH,c are proportional to the eligible population Mc κc ; per eligible person they depend on the storefront only through its income, and they are
zero at the reference income if the U.S. prices are optimal there. The vendor localises the entry
tier and leaves the frontier tier at the default in storefront c if GL,c > K ≥ GH,c and localising the
entry tier alone is worth more than localising the frontier tier alone.
(ii) A consumer who buys tier k at home buys the same tier in c if and only if pk,c′ − pk,c > χ. A
consumer who buys the frontier tier at home gains at most pL,c′ − pL,c − χ from buying the entry
tier in c, and every other cross-tier purchase gains at most the same-tier gap of the tier bought,
less χ. Prices are therefore free of arbitrage if and only if pk,c ≥ pk,c′ − χ for both paid tiers
and every home storefront c′ : arbitrage limits the dollar gap between two storefronts, by the same
amount for every tier.
(iii) If prices are free of arbitrage and the entry tier is listed gL dollars cheaper in c than in c′ , the
friction between the two storefronts is at least gL , and the arbitrage floor of any tier k in c, against
consumers from c′ , lies at least gL dollars, a proportion gL /pk,c′ , below its price in c′ . A tier whose
price in c is less than that proportion below its price in c′ is not held there by arbitrage from c′ .
Proof. (i) By (A.9), Πc /(Mc κc ) depends on the storefront only through yc , and at the reference income
the default prices are the optimum. The vendor compares four options (no override, entry tier only,
frontier tier only, both), and the stated inequalities make the entry tier alone the best. (ii) The first
statement is the definition of arbitrage for an identical product. A consumer who buys the frontier tier
at home has v ≥ (pH,c′ − pL,c′ )/(qH − qL ), so the capped tier is worth v(qH − qL ) ≥ pH,c′ − pL,c′ less to
her, and her gain from the foreign entry tier is pH,c′ − pL,c − v(qH − qL ) − χ ≤ pL,c′ − pL,c − χ. In the

Proposition A.4 assigns the two candidates different roles. Arbitrage cannot produce the pattern on
its own: a binding floor cuts every tier by the same number of dollars, a large proportional discount on
a cheap capped tier and a small one on a dear frontier tier, but it does not leave the frontier tier at the
default. And because ChatGPT Go is listed $3–4 below its U.S. price in the poorest storefronts, part (iii)
implies that arbitrage from the United States could hold a $20 tier there no closer than 15–20% to the
U.S. price, against 0.6–4% for the $100–500 tiers: arbitrage is a candidate for keeping the high-usage
tiers at the default, not the $20 tiers.4 The override cost is the candidate for the $20 tiers, and this is
where the cost share of the frontier increment matters. In the example of Appendix A.3, the further
gain GH,c from re-pricing the frontier tier, once the entry tier is localised, is 8% of the gain GL,c from
localising the entry tier at a fifth of the reference income and 3% at a tenth; at a fifth of the reference
income the ratio is 2.6, 0.49, 0.08 and 0.007 for frontier-increment cost shares of 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 and 0.6
(checks/sim_check.py). A fixed override cost therefore binds on the frontier tier and not on the entry tier
when inference cost is a large share of the frontier increment. Inference cost does not make the frontier
price uniform at the vendor’s optimum, which follows the entry price down; it makes the profit at stake
in re-pricing the frontier tier too small to justify departing from the platform’s default. Because gains
scale with the eligible population, part (i) also predicts that, at a given income, the entry tier departs
from the default more often in larger storefronts.
Remark 1 (Frontier subscribers who do not trade down). The pass-through of Proposition A.3(ii) rests
on the marginal frontier subscriber’s alternative being the entry tier. If instead the capped tier is of no
use for the tasks of frontier subscribers (qL = qF for them) while the mass market does not value the
frontier tier’s additional capability, the two tiers serve separate segments and each is priced against the
free tier. The frontier price is then a level chosen as in Proposition A.2, with ∆pH = pH,c and cost share
(cH − cF )/pH,c ; it does not respond to the entry price, and its income elasticity σH ξH /(ξH + ℓH ) is close
to zero if the cost share is high or if frontier subscribers’ valuations do not scale with local income (σH
near zero, Proposition A.2(iii)). Listed prices cannot separate this case from a held frontier price; the
sign test of Proposition A.3(iv) excludes only the unconstrained optimum of the nested menu.

Data construction and replication

Replication package. The package contains the raw extracts (World Bank API pulls, the Microsoft
dataset at commit 507c316, Anthropic Economic Index files, Google ATLAS data, ITU price baskets,
both App Store audit waves, the default-panel records, the cross-channel extracts, historical exchangerate snapshots and current exchange rates), the processing scripts, the analysis, and the figure and table
generators, together with a README that follows the template of the Social Science Data Editors. Every
number in the text can be reproduced from it: most are written to output/results.json and output/results
_wave2.json by the analysis scripts, the sensitivity analyses are written by the scripts in extensions/ and
checks/, and the rest are simple calculations from those values or are read from the output tables, the
processed data or the scripts. Re-running the pipeline from the shipped raw extracts reproduces every
table body and figure byte for byte, and independent implementations of the key regressions match the
reported estimates.
App Store audit, wave 1. Requests were made on 27 September 2026 (UTC) to https://apps.app
le.com/<cc>/app/id<app> for ChatGPT (6448311069), Claude (6473753684) and Google Gemini
(6477489729), using the two-letter codes of all 217 World Bank economies plus Taiwan. Of 654 requests, 500 returned local pages, 135 were redirected to the U.S. store and 19 returned “not found”; the
135 redirects correspond to 45 codes without a storefront of their own (Table E.4 reports them as “No
store”), and the 19 “not found” pages to apps that are not offered in a storefront. Prices were parsed from
the displayed strings, including locale-specific separators and abbreviations (e.g. Indonesian “ribu” and
“juta”). Four storefront-plan cells in which the observed page listed only an annual price were dropped.
In three cells two different candidate prices are listed for the same plan: Google AI Pro in India and
Türkiye, where one of five listings carries a lower price, and in Benin, where a 10 TB variant is listed
alongside the standard plan; the modal price is used, and taking the lowest listed price instead changes
the Google AI Pro slope by 0.0001. Re-implementing the selection rules identifies a single surviving
candidate price in every ChatGPT Go, ChatGPT Plus and Claude Pro storefront-plan cell, and annualprice exclusion cutoffs of three, five and eight times the lowest listed price select the same listings. The
first wave stored the parsed listing records, not the pages. The first-wave regressions include the U.S.
storefront, so their samples (162–165 storefronts by app) differ by one from the second-wave samples,
which exclude it.
App Store audit, wave 2, and the page archive. On 16 October 2026 the same request was made
for 34 apps in all 218 codes (7,412 requests; requests that returned HTTP 429 were repeated until a page or a definitive status was obtained, and the last definitive record per storefront and app
is used). Apple’s product page had changed from an embedded JSON record to an HTML list between the waves; both formats are parsed, and the full pages of the three assistants and the in-app
purchase block of every other page were stored compressed, each with the SHA-256 fingerprint of the
page as received, so that the listing behind every parsed record can be inspected and the extraction replayed. The fingerprints travel with the parsed records in the package; the page archive is deposited as
appstore_wave2_page_archive_2026-10-16.zip in the Zenodo record of version 3.1: 12,860 files
covering the 12,225 pages with HTTP 200, 109 MB, and the hashes of all 635 full pages match the
parsed records. Plans that OpenAI renamed between the waves (Pro 5× and 20× to Pro 100 and Pro

44

200) are matched by their position in the menu, and the new Pro 500 tier is recorded but has no first-wave
counterpart. Items are classified as consumables when their name denotes credits, gems, boosts, packs
or similar one-off purchases, and as subscriptions otherwise. The monthly price of a subscription is the
modal price among its listed variants after excluding variants priced at five or more times the lowest,
the same rule as in the first wave; because the product page lists at most ten purchases, the monthly
variant can be missing from a full list while an annual variant remains, so a price recorded as monthly
is dropped when it exceeds three times the platform’s reference for the item’s U.S. monthly price (the
storefront multiplier of the default panel times the U.S. price, or three times the U.S. price itself for
single-variant listings in storefronts without a multiplier). The guard flags 151 of 21,938 item-storefront
cells with a U.S. price and income data (157 before the income restriction), in 14 items, 129 of them in
Super Duolingo, Headspace Plus, Gemini’s 100 GB plan, Character.AI+ and Evernote Plus (Table E.11);
a storefront whose monthly variant is censored is thereby missing rather than mispriced. Items that the
same firm lists under two apps or two spellings are counted once in the item-level statistics (five of 152
items), keeping the listing with more storefronts.
Estimated platform schedule. For each of the 30 small-developer apps and each storefront the
monthly price of every subscription item is recorded, and items are keyed by their U.S. price point. For
storefront c and U.S. price point P, the schedule price is the modal local price among the items at that
point when at least three items are observed and the mode is unique and accounts for at least half of
them; 1,551 of 2,414 storefront-point cells satisfy this, and 22 cells are two-way ties at exactly one half,
which yield no schedule price. The storefront multiplier is the median of the schedule price divided by
P over points with P ≥ $2.99, computed for storefronts with at least three such points (164); its spread,
the range of the point-wise ratios divided by the median, is below 0.2 in 111 storefronts, which form
the clean subsample (108 with income data). A plan’s reference price is the exact schedule price for its
U.S. price point when one exists, and otherwise the multiplier times its U.S. price; Table 5 reports the
share of each plan’s storefronts with an exact reference. Resolving ties by file order, by the lower price
or by the higher price instead changes the schedule’s slope within 0.0325–0.0329 and the ChatGPT Go
deviation slope within 0.110–0.111; discarding every cell whose mode accounts for exactly half of the
items changes them to 0.027 and 0.110 (Table 6). The multiplier divided by the market exchange rate
is the tax factor that the platform applies, which is one in the median U.S.-dollar storefront and above
one in every European storefront priced in its own currency (1.19–1.36 in 31 of the 34 European and
Central Asian storefronts outside the dollar, and 1.05–1.12 in Romania, Cyprus and Montenegro), in
line with their value-added tax rates and Apple’s rounding to local price conventions. The vintage test
of Section 5.3 uses the range of the log exchange rate of each storefront currency against the dollar over
six dates between March 2024 and October 2026 (data/raw/fx_hist/).

Prices in other channels: Google Play and Microsoft’s web store

The audit observes one channel. If the App Store prices of the AI plans were specific to that channel, for
instance because an in-app price list adds the channel’s own tax or fee, the same plan would be priced
differently on the web or on Google Play; if they are the prices the vendor sets for the country, the
channels would agree. Two extracts collected on the day of the second wave, 16 October 2026, allow
the comparison for the plans whose prices other channels display (Section 4); Table C.1 summarises it,
and Table C.2 lists the Google Play comparison by country.
Microsoft’s web store. The page https://www.microsoft.com/<locale>/microsoft-365/buy/compare
-all-microsoft-365-products lists the monthly and annual prices of Microsoft 365 Personal, Family and
Premium. Of 107 locale paths tried, 63 returned prices, covering 57 countries (several countries appear
under two locales with identical prices); 42 returned no page, among them Nigeria, Kenya, Pakistan,
Egypt and Bangladesh, where the product pages exist but show no price, so the web store does not sell
the consumer plans there; Russia lists the products without prices; and the Chinese page offers Personal
and Family only. The prices follow the locale path rather than the requesting address, since every page’s
currency matched its country, and the pages do not state whether prices include tax. Within a country
the three plans share a currency and a tax treatment, so the ratio of the Personal to the Premium price,
relative to the same ratio in the United States, is a default-free measure in the same spirit as Section 5.1:
its income slope across the 54 countries with both plans and income data is 0.082 (HC1 s.e. 0.018), the
ratio is 31% below the U.S. ratio in India, the only low- or lower-middle-income country with a web

Google Play. The Play web page of an app, requested with the country parameter gl, shows the range

The public usage indicators: tables and figures

This appendix holds the estimates summarised in Section 6: the income gradient of any use (Table D.1,
Figure D.1, with controls in Table D.2), the comparison of any use with per-capita Claude activity
(Figure D.3, Tables D.4, D.5 and D.6), and the accounting split of the any-use gradient into an internetuse component and the ratio of AI users to internet users (Table D.3, Figure D.2).
Any use. On country-specific estimates, a one-log-point increase in income is associated with 8.3 percentage points higher use, and the elasticity is 0.370 (s.e. 0.021; R2 = 0.71). Including pooled regional
values lowers the elasticity to 0.336; collapsing each pool to one observation restores it (0.367). The
elasticity is unchanged by controls for internet use, tertiary enrolment, urbanisation, age structure, digital payments and region fixed effects (0.355–0.367), and conditional on income none of the controls
is individually significant except urbanisation in columns (3)–(4) of Table D.2 (p ≈ 0.04), which loses
significance once region fixed effects are added. Between the first half of 2025 and the second quarter
of 2026 the median user share rose by 5.5 points in the high-income group, 3.7 in the upper-middle-, 2.0
in the lower-middle- and 0.9 in the low-income group (Figure D.1b): proportional growth was similar
across the three higher income groups (21–26%) and slightly faster in lagging economies (−0.026 per
log point of the initial share, s.e. 0.011), while in percentage points each log point of income is associated with a 1.37-point larger gain (s.e. 0.13), which reconciles reports of faster proportional growth in
lower-income countries (Chatterji et al., 2025) with reports of a widening divide (Microsoft AI Economy
Institute, 2026a; Liu et al., 2025).
The accounting split. Define the ratio Rc = Dc /Ic , where Dc is Microsoft’s working-age AI-use share
and Ic is the all-age internet-use share, so that ln Dc = ln Ic + ln Rc exactly. Because Microsoft’s estimator
scales device telemetry by internet penetration, Rc backs that scaling out and is best read as the telemetry
signal per online user rather than as an observed probability of adopting AI conditional on being online;
the denominator also has a different age base (all ages, against 15–64 for Dc ) and often a different year,
and in lower-income economies with young populations the all-age rate understates the working-age
rate, which raises Rc there relative to older, richer economies. In the 109-economy estimation sample
no ratio exceeds one. The slopes on log income are 0.182 for internet use and 0.189 for the ratio,
summing to 0.371 against the directly estimated slope of 0.370 (a rounding difference), so the internetuse component accounts for 49% of the gradient as an accounting matter; holding income fixed, the
elasticity of AI use with respect to internet use is 0.05 (s.e. 0.20). Across groups (Table D.3, Figure D.2)
the connectivity share of the gap to high-income economies rises from 21% for upper-middle-income to
49% for lower-middle-income and 67% for the three low-income economies with country-specific data.
The “AI / internet” column of Table D.3 reports the arithmetic mean of the country-level ratios within
each group (37.5% in high-income economies and 22.7%, 23.6% and 23.0% from the upper-middle- to
the low-income group), whereas the log-gap columns are differences of group means of logs; the ratio’s
income slope across non-high-income economies is −0.03 (s.e. 0.06). The low-income row contains
only three economies.
Per-capita Claude activity. Anthropic’s own report documents an income gradient of Claude activity
near 0.7 using GDP per working-age capita (Appel et al., 2025); the contribution here is the comparison

The ChatGPT Go rollout: a descriptive record

OpenAI’s release notes and press reports date the arrival of ChatGPT Go in India (19 August 2025),
Indonesia (22 September 2025), sixteen further Asian economies (8 October 2025: Afghanistan,
Bangladesh, Bhutan, Brunei, Cambodia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan,
Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Timor-Leste and Viet Nam), 71 undisclosed economies (14 October
2025), Brazil (28 October) and eight European economies (30 October), after which further economies
followed before the worldwide rollout of 16 January 2026 (OpenAI, 2026d, 2025; Mehta, 2025). India
also received twelve months of the tier at no charge from 4 November 2025, and in the same months
Indian subscribers of Airtel and Jio received Perplexity Pro (from July 2025) and Google AI Pro (from
October 2025) at no charge; app-analytics firms recorded a ninefold rise in Perplexity’s downloads in
India while ChatGPT’s and Claude’s stayed flat (Singh, 2026). The rollout record, with sources, is
data/raw/openai/chatgpt_go_rollout.csv.
Against these dates the public indicators offer four semi-annual or quarterly windows of any use
(H1 2025 before any rollout; H2 2025, in which India, Indonesia and the sixteen Asian economies were
exposed for 2.8–4.4 months and most others for at most 2.6; Q1 and Q2 2026 with the tier universal)
and five windows of Claude activity (4–11 August 2025, before India’s launch; 13–20 November 2025;
February, April and May 2026). Table F.1 and Figure F.1 describe how the early economies’ indicators
moved relative to the later-exposed ones. For each window the change in the log indicator is regressed on
log income and the initial log level in the economies that did not receive the tier early, every economy’s
residual change is computed from those coefficients, and the residual of India and the mean residual of
the sixteen Asian economies are reported with their rank among all residuals and, for the sixteen, with
the coefficient on a cohort indicator. Because all sixteen economies, and India and Indonesia, lie in South
and South-East Asia, the specification with World Bank region fixed effects is the one reported first; the
specification without them was written into the analysis script before the outcomes were computed and
was not registered externally, and the region-fixed-effects specification was added afterwards.

The record shows the following. With region fixed effects, in the half-year in which the sixteen Asian
economies received the tier a week before 71 other economies and two to three months before the rest,
their share of adults using any generative-AI product grew 0.021 log points faster than income, starting

zero, and the later windows are smaller still. Without region fixed effects the same contrasts are 0.024
(s.e. 0.010; 2.5% faster growth), a difference exceeded by one of 2,000 random groups of comparison
economies, with ten of the eleven economies showing positive residuals, and −0.180 (s.e. 0.063; a
16.5% relative fall), with eleven of fourteen residuals negative. India grew 4.9% faster than predicted in
any use (0.048 log points), a residual exceeded in absolute value by 8% of economies, and its Claude
activity fell 15.3% relative to others between August and November 2025 (−0.166 log points), a residual
that 39% of economies exceed; Indonesia’s residuals are small.
This record does not support a causal interpretation, for reasons the table makes visible. The comparison group received the tier within six days to three weeks of the sixteen, so the H2 2025 contrast
is a difference in exposure of a few weeks within a six-month window. The early cohorts lie in one
region, and the region fixed effects that this geography requires double the standard errors and leave no
contrast distinguishable from zero. The rollout order was chosen by the vendor, launches came with
marketing, India’s free year coincided with two telecom bundles that gave other frontier tiers away, and
the only pre-period is a single half-year, so parallel trends cannot be checked. The episode is recorded
here as an account of what the public indicators show around the dates, and as the kind of variation that a
design with higher-frequency outcomes, registered specifications and estimators for staggered adoption
(Callaway and Sant’Anna, 2021) could exploit.

A protocol for recording access conditions

A recurring fee does not determine the cost of completing useful work: an unchanged fee can correspond
to different entitlements by account cohort and date, and a new tier can add eligibility rights rather
than replace a price, as the tariff change of 29 September 2026 illustrated (Section 2). Table G.1 lists
the fields that a reproducible observation of access conditions should record, as a proposed extension
of the present audit rather than a newly collected dataset; the replication package supplies the field
dictionary (checks/access_measurement_fields.csv). Missing terms should remain missing rather than
be assigned a provider-wide constant, and a prospective evaluation of what a task costs should report
total expenditure and success rates alongside cost per successful task, since excluding failed attempts
would understate costs.
