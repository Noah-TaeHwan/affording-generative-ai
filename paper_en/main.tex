\begin{abstract}
\noindent Generative AI is sold on a subscription menu that is almost the same everywhere, while national incomes differ a hundredfold. This paper asks which part of the menu vendors price for local incomes, what that implies for affordability, and whether a cheaper local tier brings more people in. A one-day audit of the tax-inclusive prices listed for nine plans of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini in 170 national Apple App Store storefronts shows that vendors localise the entry tier only. The local price of the \$20 standard plans barely varies with income (an income slope of 0.03--0.04, against 0.23 if prices tracked local price levels), whereas OpenAI's entry tier is partly localised (0.14), so the standard plan costs 2.5 times the entry plan in the United States and 4 times in the median low-income storefront. A \$20 plan therefore costs 0.65\% of per-capita income a year in the median high-income economy and 29\% in the median low-income one, and 60\% of the covered working-age population lives where it exceeds the 2\% broadband benchmark. Around the staggered rollout of ChatGPT Go, economies that received the tier first saw the share of adults using generative AI grow 2--5\% faster than income and starting level predict, then a relative fall in activity on a competing product; the differences are small, confined to the exposure window and not separable from regional trends. Counterfactuals show that localising or subsidising the frontier tier would change affordability far more than vendors' own localisation does.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} generative AI; international price discrimination; versioning; affordability; digital divide; technology diffusion.\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} O33, O15, L11, L86, D63, F63.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Generative AI is sold to individuals on a subscription menu that is almost identical across countries: a free tier, an entry tier, a \$20 standard tier and high-usage tiers at about \$100 and \$200 a month (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). National incomes, by contrast, differ by two orders of magnitude. The same \$20 is 0.3\% of per-capita income in the United States and more than 40\% in Mozambique. Whether the productivity gains documented for people who use these tools \citep{brynjolfsson2025generative,noy2023experimental,dellacqua2023navigating,peng2023impact} can reach poorer countries therefore depends in part on a pricing question that has received little systematic attention: when AI firms sell the same service everywhere, which part of it do they price for local incomes, and does a cheaper local tier bring more people into use?

This paper answers the first question with a dated audit of the tax-inclusive local prices that Apple's App Store listed on 27 September 2026 for nine plans of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini in every national storefront (218 codes, 170 with local price lists), and the second, as far as public data allow, with the staggered rollout of OpenAI's low-price ChatGPT Go tier, which reached India in August 2025, Indonesia in September, sixteen further Asian economies and 71 others in October, and the rest of the world in January 2026. The audit shows that vendors localise the entry tier but not frontier access. The local dollar price of the \$20 standard plans hardly varies with income: the income slope is 0.039 for ChatGPT Plus, 0.026 for Claude Pro and 0.037 for Google AI Pro, against 0.23 if prices tracked local price levels and 1 under proportional pricing. ChatGPT Go, by contrast, is partly localised (slope 0.143), so that within the same storefront, where exchange rates and taxes cancel, the standard plan costs 2.5 times the entry plan in the United States, 2.9 times in the median high-income storefront and 4.0 times in the median low-income one. Every storefront in a low-income economy prices in U.S. dollars, and 36\% of low-income economies have no local storefront at all. Burdens follow mechanically: a \$20 plan costs 0.65\% of per-capita income a year in the median high-income economy, 3.1\% in upper-middle-, 8.7\% in lower-middle- and 29\% in low-income economies, and 60\% of the working-age population of the economies covered lives where it exceeds the 2\% benchmark that the ITU and the UN Broadband Commission apply to broadband. For the richest fifth of households in the median low-income economy, one plan would still cost 13\% of average income.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it provides a dated, multi-provider, cross-country record of generative-AI subscription prices, archived with its code so that later audits can measure change. Earlier work divided one U.S. price by national incomes \citep{sathish2024llempower}; this audit adds local rather than U.S. prices, several providers and tiers, and a record of retrieval outcomes, currencies and plan matching. Second, it shows how AI vendors price-discriminate across countries: through versioning, cutting the price of capability-limited tiers while keeping frontier tiers at global prices, a pattern in line with second-degree price discrimination \citep{mussa1978monopoly,goldfarb2019digital}. The affordability gap is therefore concentrated in paid access to frontier capability rather than in access to AI as such. Third, it provides first evidence on whether a localised entry tier changes adoption, and sets that evidence against a simple model in which a free tier separates the decision to use AI from the decision to pay for more capability (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel}). In the half-year in which sixteen Asian economies received ChatGPT Go ahead of most others (a week before a batch of 71 economies and up to three months before the rest), the share of adults using any generative-AI product grew 2--3\% faster there than income and starting level predict, and in India, which received the tier first and a free year of it from November 2025, 5\% faster; the differences vanish in the quarters after the tier became universal, and activity on a competing product (Claude) fell by about a sixth in the sixteen economies relative to others. These estimates are small, rest on four semi-annual or quarterly windows and are not distinguishable from regional trends, so they are a first look rather than a test. Public usage indicators also show that any use is only moderately income-graded (elasticity 0.37, half of it coinciding with internet use) whereas per-capita Claude activity is twice as income-graded (0.71), a contrast that the free-tier model can produce but that vendor choice, usage intensity and measurement can produce as well. Throughout, a conditional framework with a free and a paid tier (Proposition~\ref{prop:margins}, Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel}) organises the evidence, and the price-schedule counterfactuals of Section~\ref{sec:policy} translate the estimates into the magnitudes that pricing and subsidy policies would have to move.

For policy, the findings point to paid access to frontier capability as the margin on which affordability policy would bite. Counterfactuals computed from the audit show that localising the \$20 tier as the entry tier is localised would lower the median low-income burden from 29\% to 15\% of per-capita income, indexing it to local price levels would lower it to 11\%, and bringing it to the broadband benchmark would require a subsidy of 93\% of the price in the median low-income economy and 35\% in the median upper-middle-income economy, at a fiscal cost of 1.5\% and 0.1\% of GNI if one tenth of the working-age population were covered. Institutional licences, usage-capped local pricing of frontier tiers and public procurement are the instruments these magnitudes favour; cheap entry tiers, on the evidence here, move use at most modestly.

The paper relates to several literatures. Studies of who adopts generative AI within countries find adoption skewed toward the young, the educated and higher earners \citep{bick2024rapid,humlum2025unequal}; cross-country studies based on web traffic, telemetry or conversation data document large gaps by income \citep{liu2024who,liu2025who,misra2025measuring,chatterji2025how,appel2025uneven,daepp2026how,iscenko2026atlas}; and direct surveys are beginning to distinguish free from paid use \citep{kanzamanova2026digital}. Earlier work on computers, the internet and mobile telephony identified income, human capital and prices as the main correlates of diffusion \citep{caselli2001cross,chinn2007determinants,aker2010mobile,bjorkegren2019adoption}, and the diffusion literature has long distinguished the timing of adoption from the intensity of use \citep{comin2004cross,comin2010exploration}: \citet{comin2018technology} show that for earlier technologies adoption lags converged across countries while usage intensity diverged. Studies of occupational exposure to AI \citep{felten2021occupational,eloundou2024gpts,cazzaniga2024genai,gmyrek2025generative} supply the exposure figures used below, and development-oriented assessments \citep{korinek2021artificial,undp2025hdr,unctad2025tir,worldbank2026wdr} frame the policy stakes. The affordability convention for telecommunications \citep{itu2025ff} supplies the benchmark extended here, and the literature on international pricing and product versioning \citep{goldberg1997goods,cavallo2014currency,mussa1978monopoly,goldfarb2019digital} frames the audit, although taxes, platform commissions and product differences mean that the estimated slopes are not firms' price-discrimination parameters.

The analysis is descriptive. Because a uniform dollar price makes the burden a monotone transformation of income, a cross-country association between burdens and use cannot identify a price effect; the audit is one day's listing in one payment channel; and the rollout comparison observes exposure differences of a few weeks across a few windows, with a rollout order chosen by the vendor. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} sets out what a full evaluation of pricing and access policies would require.

Section~\ref{sec:background} describes the price menu and the subsequent tariff change. Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the burden measure and presents the framework. Section~\ref{sec:data} documents the data. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports what vendors localise, the burdens, the usage indicators, the rollout comparison and the policy counterfactuals. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses interpretation, convergence, an evaluation agenda, policy and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The formal model, data construction, connectivity decomposition, additional tables, a \$500 reference-fee scenario and a measurement protocol are in the appendices.

\emph{Convergence.} The three menus have converged on nearly the same rungs: a free tier, an entry tier at \$5--8, a \$20 standard tier, and tiers at about \$100 and \$200. Using several assistants multiplies the cost: a professional who keeps the \$20 tier of all three pays about \$60 a month.

\emph{Quantity, not intelligence.} The upper rungs are differentiated primarily by usage allowances (``5$\times$'' and ``20$\times$'' the standard tier) and, secondarily, by access to the most capable model variants and features. With near-zero marginal cost of replication but non-trivial inference costs, digital goods lend themselves to versioning \citep{goldfarb2019digital}, and the menu is a textbook instance of second-degree price discrimination by quantity and quality \citep{mussa1978monopoly}. The free and entry tiers are degraded versions of the standard tier: ChatGPT Go, for instance, offers an ``Instant'' model with lower caps, while Plus offers the ``Thinking'' model with higher limits \citep{openai2026go}. A higher price therefore buys more frontier capability per month, although neither the plan name nor the price is a comparable measure of useful capability across vendors, languages or tasks.

\emph{Localisation happens at the bottom of the ladder.} The entry tiers are the vendors' instrument for reaching lower-income markets: ChatGPT Go launched in India in August 2025 and was rolled out to 170 further countries before its U.S. launch at \$8 in January 2026 \citep{openai2026go}. Section~\ref{sec:prices} shows that it is the only rung whose local price varies materially with income. Promotional give-aways, such as bundles of premium plans with mobile subscriptions in India, further lower effective prices in some markets, but they are temporary and country-specific and are not captured by a listing audit.

\subsection{The tariff change of 29 September 2026}\label{sec:tariff}

Two days after the audit, OpenAI announced a Pro 500 tier at \$500 a month, sold initially on the web and including its fastest service mode, and reopened Pro 200 subscriptions with a lower included allowance for new subscribers, while existing subscribers keep their allowance until 29 October 2026 \citep{openai2026releasenotes,openai2026prorevision}. The episode illustrates two measurement points. An unchanged fee can correspond to different entitlements by account cohort and date, and a new tier can add eligibility rights rather than replace a price. A tariff is therefore a bundle $(F,\mathbf{A},\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{w})$ of fee, included allowances, eligible models and service modes, overage terms and reset rules, of which the burden defined below uses the fee $F$ alone. Quality-adjusted prices in the model market fall rapidly \citep{andre2025markets}; they are a different object from the recurring cash commitment of a subscription, and neither identifies the cash cost of completing a given task. The change postdates both the price audit and the usage windows, so it is treated separately: Appendix~\ref{app:500} reports a \$500 reference-fee scenario, and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} sets out a protocol for recording the elements of a tariff that the fee alone omits.

\section{Framework}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The affordability burden}

\begin{definition}[AI affordability burden]
For a plan with monthly price $p_c$ in country $c$ (current U.S. dollars) and per-capita income $y_c$ (current U.S. dollars),
\begin{equation}
\AAB_c(p_c) = \frac{12\,p_c}{y_c}\times 100 .
\label{eq:aab}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The burden is a normalised price: it states how large the price is relative to average income. It is not a budget share and does not measure what anyone spends. The denominator is Atlas-method GNI per capita, which smooths exchange rates over three years. The measure follows the convention of the ITU's broadband affordability statistics, which express the monthly price of an entry-level basket as a share of monthly GNI per capita and assess it against the 2\% benchmark of the UN Broadband Commission \citep{itu2025ff}. I use that benchmark as a borrowed reference and report sensitivity to other cutoffs. For a cutoff $t$ in percentage points the threshold income is $y_c\geq1200p/t$; at 2\% this is \$12,000 for a \$20 plan and \$4,800 for an \$8 plan.

Because average income conceals distribution, I also compute burdens at quintile means, approximating the mean income of quintile $q$ by $y_{cq}=y_c s_{cq}/0.2$, where $s_{cq}$ is the quintile's survey-based income or consumption share. These are synthetic resource levels rather than measured disposable incomes, since survey welfare concepts, survey dates and national accounts differ. GNI also includes income that does not accrue to households, so burdens relative to household final consumption per capita are reported as well.

\begin{remark}[What a PPP denominator measures]\label{rem:ppp}
For a service priced in dollars, the burden relative to income at market exchange rates is the relevant cash-cost quantity. Dividing the dollar price by income at purchasing-power parity instead yields $12p/y^{PPP}_c = 12\,(p\cdot PLR_c)/y_c$, where $PLR_c = y_c/y^{PPP}_c$, the ratio of Atlas to PPP income, is a proxy for the price level relative to the United States.\footnote{Because the Atlas method averages exchange rates over three years, the ratio differs from the same-year GDP price-level ratio by a median of 3.1\% (90th percentile 8.3\%) across 197 economies. Its income elasticity is 0.228, against 0.224 for the GDP price-level ratio.} The same algebra equals the Atlas-income burden under a constructed price $p_c=p\cdot PLR_c$, that is, the burden that would obtain if the vendor indexed its price to local price levels. Because price levels rise with income \citep{balassa1964purchasing,samuelson1964theoretical}, and PPP conversion factors are designed for broad consumption baskets rather than internationally priced digital services \citep{deaton2010understanding}, the PPP-based ratio is best read as this counterfactual pricing scenario rather than as a robustness check on the same quantity. Section~\ref{sec:prices} compares actual pricing with it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A free tier and two margins of access}\label{sec:mechanism}

A free tier permits initial use while a paid tier offers more capability. In the three-option model of Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel}, connected individuals with heterogeneous valuations choose non-use, free use or paid use. When the threshold for upgrading lies above the threshold for using the free tier, the decision to use AI at all is governed by non-monetary hurdles (connectivity, skills, language) and the paid price drops out of it, whereas paying for more capability depends on the ratio of price to income. Proposition~\ref{prop:margins} in Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel} then predicts a steeper income gradient on the paid margin than on the margin of any use whenever prices are not localised, and the difference falls as localisation increases. If the paid option is attractive enough to draw users directly from non-use, the paid price affects both margins. The model conditions on common participation costs and service levels. It organises the evidence and generates predictions; the public usage data do not observe paid conversion, so the model is not fitted, and the rollout comparison of Section~\ref{sec:rollout} confronts only its extensive-margin prediction.

\subsection{What the public usage indicators identify}
\label{sec:usage_measurement}

The paid-adoption share of the model is not observed in the public data. Microsoft's AI User Share is an estimated adoption rate for a set of tracked products. Anthropic's AI Usage Index is a population-normalised measure of Claude conversation activity that pools free and paid accounts; it does not identify the number of paid users or their usage separately. The empirical comparison therefore concerns two observed diffusion indicators, not separately measured extensive and paid margins.

An accounting identity shows the distinction. In a common period and population,
let $N_c$ denote population, $U_c$ any-AI users, $C_c$ Claude users and $M_c$
Claude conversations, with Claude users a subset of any-AI users. Define
$D_c^*=U_c/N_c$, $s_c=C_c/U_c$ and $m_c=M_c/C_c$. For positive counts,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{M_c}{N_c}=D_c^*s_cm_c,\qquad
 AUI_c=K D_c^*s_cm_c,\qquad K=\frac{N_W}{M_W},
 \label{eq:aui_identity}
\end{equation}
where $W$ is the common reference universe used to normalise the index.
Consequently, under aligned definitions,
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_{AUI}=\varepsilon_{D^*}+\varepsilon_s+\varepsilon_m.
 \label{eq:aui_elasticity_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The same additive relation holds for ordinary least-squares log-income slopes
estimated on an identical country sample. The country-invariant normalisation
$K$ affects the intercept rather than the cross-country slope.

A steeper conversation-activity gradient can thus reflect an income gradient
in Claude's share of AI users, in conversations per Claude user, or both.
Neither component is synonymous with paid access: with $\pi_c$ the paid share of Claude users and $m_{F,c},m_{P,c}$ the mean conversation counts of free and paid users, $m_c=(1-\pi_c)m_{F,c}+\pi_cm_{P,c}$, and the index identifies none of these terms. In practice the two sources also do not provide the harmonised counts of equation~\eqref{eq:aui_identity}: Microsoft's estimate incorporates telemetry coverage, scaling and shrinkage, while Anthropic uses sampled conversations, publication thresholds and release-specific product coverage, so the observed gradient difference can reflect measurement and selection as well as behaviour. The empirical analysis accordingly labels its outcomes any-product adoption and per-capita Claude activity, and reads their contrast as consistent with the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} rather than as a test of it. Testing paid upgrading would require country-level user counts by subscription status, usage quantities and effective prices, preferably around a plausibly exogenous change in access conditions.

\paragraph{Income.} The main denominator is GNI per capita (Atlas method) for 2025, available for 182 economies. For the remaining 19 I use the latest value from 2022--2024. Nigeria's 2022 GNI per capita, for instance, has been revised from \$2,140 to \$2,940 in the current WDI vintage, while its 2025 value is \$1,360 after the naira's depreciation. I use the official FY2027 groups throughout. For Argentina (\$14,650) and T\"urkiye (\$16,300) the 2025 values in the July 2026 WDI update exceed the high-income threshold, although both are classified as upper-middle income; the World Bank keeps them there because the Atlas method does not fully smooth their persistent high inflation \citep{worldbank2026class}.

\paragraph{Local prices.} For each of 218 two-letter storefront codes and each app, I retrieved the App Store product page and extracted the storefront currency and the list of in-app purchases with their displayed prices (the list's title is localised, e.g.\ ``In-App-Käufe''). Plans are identified by name. Where a plan appears with monthly and annual variants, the monthly price is the modal price after discarding variants priced at five or more times the lowest one. The archive retains parsed listing records rather than the original HTML; billing-period inference from names and price magnitudes is a matching heuristic, not independently confirmed SKU metadata. Prices are converted to dollars at market rates of 27 September 2026 from open.er-api.com, which agree with an independent source to within 1.6\%. Requests redirected to the U.S. storefront are coded as no local listing observed (45 codes). Pages returning ``not found'' are coded as unavailable in the audit (ChatGPT: Belarus, China, Hong Kong SAR, Macao SAR, Russia and Venezuela; Claude: the same plus Afghanistan, Myanmar and Yemen; Gemini: Afghanistan, Belarus, China and Russia). App Store prices include value-added or goods-and-services taxes in almost all storefronts and exclude sales taxes in the United States and Canada. They can also exceed web prices because of platform commissions (Claude Max, for example, costs \$124.99 in the U.S. App Store against ``from \$100'' on the web). The audit therefore measures listed prices in one platform-specific payment channel. These are not the vendor's web list prices, and they are not necessarily the amounts charged at checkout, which introductory offers or legacy price points can change.

\paragraph{Diffusion.} The Microsoft AI User Share is the estimated share of people aged 15--64 who used a generative-AI product during the period, derived from anonymised Windows telemetry on visits to 19 AI services, scaled for device and operating-system market shares, mobile use and internet penetration, and shrunk toward the global average for countries whose users mostly opt out of telemetry \citep{misra2025measuring}. Two features of the series affect the analysis. First, economies with insufficient telemetry or populations below two million are pooled into regional aggregates: 36 of the 147 economies share identical series with their neighbours, and they coincide with the economies flagged as ``region-imputed'' in the technical paper. The pooled groups include 20 of the 23 low-income economies in the data (for example, Benin, Ghana, Niger, Nigeria and eight other West African economies all record 8.7\%, 9.3\%, 10.1\% and 10.4\% in the four periods). Country-specific estimates are therefore available for only three low-income economies (The Gambia, Rwanda and Syria). I use country-specific estimates as the main sample and show results with pooled values, and with each pool collapsed to one observation, as robustness checks. Second, Microsoft cautions that several widely used domestic tools, notably in China, are not yet covered \citep{microsoft2026q2}.

The Anthropic AI Usage Index for country $c$ is the country's share of Claude.ai conversations divided by its share of the world's working-age population, from privacy-preserving samples of conversations, about one million per one-week window in the 2025 and February 2026 releases and a fixed number per hour over two calendar months in the June 2026 release \citep{handa2025which,appel2025uneven,appel2026primitives}. Later releases include consumer Free, Pro and Max accounts without distinguishing their country-level contributions; those account types were already included in the November 2025 sample. The consumer series excludes API and enterprise traffic and is published only for countries meeting release-specific thresholds. I harmonise the index normalisation across releases, without making changing product coverage comparable (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). Google's ATLAS reports a per-capita adoption quintile for Gemini products in April 2026 \citep{iscenko2026atlas}. The releases differ in window, sampling and product coverage; Appendix~\ref{app:data} tabulates them.

\paragraph{Entry-tier rollout.} OpenAI's release notes and press reports date the arrival of ChatGPT Go in India (19 August 2025), Indonesia (22 September 2025) and sixteen further Asian economies (8 October 2025), after which 71 undisclosed economies followed on 14 October, Brazil and eight European economies on 28--30 October, further economies before January, and the rest of the world on 16 January 2026 \citep{openai2026go,openai2025gorollout,techcrunch2025go}. India also received twelve months of the tier at no charge from 4 November 2025. The rollout record, with sources, is part of the replication package.

\paragraph{Computational workflow.} Generative AI tools, ChatGPT and Codex (OpenAI) and Claude (Anthropic), assisted with code development, extraction review and drafting; the author verified the sources, analysis and text. The replication package (Appendix~\ref{app:data}) contains the frozen inputs, scripts and outputs. Re-running it reproduces every number in the paper, and independent reimplementations of the key regressions in plain NumPy match the reported estimates.

\subsection{Estimands and inference}

For plan $k$, the price regression is $\ln p_{ck}=\alpha_k+\beta_k\ln y_c+u_{ck}$, estimated by ordinary least squares with equal country weights. The coefficient is the cross-country association of listed consumer prices with income within the observed sample; it is not a demand elasticity, and it is not a firm's localisation rule, because taxes, platform commissions and product differences all enter the listing. Because $\ln\AAB_{ck}=\ln(1200)+\ln p_{ck}-\ln y_c$, the burden slope on the same sample is exactly $\beta_k-1$, and a constant reference price imposes a slope of $-1$ by construction; regressing use on that burden would supply no price variation independent of income. The matched Go-to-Plus regression uses the ratio of two listings observed in the same storefront and then compares ratios across economies; it cancels the currency conversion common to both plans but supplies no within-country variation over time.

Usage regressions take the form $\ln U_c=\alpha+\eta\ln y_c+X_c'\gamma+e_c$. Common-sample comparisons use the same countries and income observations for both outcomes. HC3 standard errors describe regression uncertainty conditional on the published indicators; currency-clustered errors are reported for the price regressions, and country-clustered and bootstrap intervals for the difference between usage gradients. None of these incorporates the estimation uncertainty inside the providers' own indicators or selection into publication. Population weighting is reported as a sensitivity because it describes a different estimand.

The audit date, income year, diffusion window and survey year are distinct, and the cross-sectional design neither links contemporaneous price changes to later use nor estimates individual willingness to pay. The rollout comparison of Section~\ref{sec:rollout} is a case comparison with permutation inference, specified before the outcomes were examined: for each window it regresses the change in the log indicator on log income and the initial level in the economies that did not receive the tier early, and reports the residual change of the early economies with its rank among all residuals.

The pattern is consistent with the logic of menu design, although the audit does not identify vendors' motives. One possible explanation is arbitrage: localising the \$20 tier would invite high-valuation users to buy through foreign accounts, whereas localising a capability-limited entry tier does not, because high-valuation users self-select into the higher tier \citep{mussa1978monopoly}; platform price schedules, taxes and market-entry sequencing could produce the same listings. Whatever the reason, the vendors that price-discriminate across countries sell degraded quality cheaply but barely localise the prices of frontier tiers. By Proposition~\ref{prop:margins}, price should then matter most on the paid margin, which remains unlocalised. Taxes, platform rules and currency schedules also enter the listings, so the slopes are associations in a price menu rather than firms' discrimination parameters, and the audit cannot establish whether a lower-allowance tier suffices for any particular task.

\paragraph{Robustness: currency dependence and a matched-plan comparison.} Prices in the same currency share exchange-rate and platform-pricing shocks, and the currency clusters are highly unbalanced: the Plus sample has 102 USD listings, 25 EUR listings and 37 singleton currencies. Currency-clustered standard errors are accordingly larger than HC3 errors (Table~\ref{tab:price_revision}). For ChatGPT Plus the clustered 95\% interval is $[-0.0002, 0.079]$, so the small positive standard-tier slope is not distinguishable from zero under that dependence assumption, which only strengthens the uniformity finding; the Go slope's clustered interval, $[0.114, 0.173]$, excludes zero. A matched comparison removes currency conversion altogether. Within each storefront, the log Go-to-Plus price ratio has an income slope of 0.104 (currency-clustered s.e.\ 0.007; 164 economies), which on a common sample is exactly the difference between the Go and Plus slopes. Its value is not new information but a check: because the ratio cancels the exchange-rate conversion and any proportional tax common to both plans, the entry-tier gradient is not an artefact of currency conversion. It remains 0.113 within the 102 USD-priced storefronts, 0.112 without them and 0.125 without USD and EUR (Table~\ref{tab:menu_sensitivity} and Figure~\ref{fig:price_ratio}), although no low-income economy remains once USD listings are excluded, so the exclusion samples speak to middle- and high-income markets. Restricting the separate regressions to economies with 2025 income data gives slopes of 0.149 for Go and 0.046 for Plus.

\subsection{Burdens}\label{sec:burdens}

With prices this uniform, burdens are set by income. Table~\ref{tab:burden} reports median burdens by income group for common reference prices from \$8 to \$200 a month. At \$20 the median burden is 0.65\% of per-capita income in high-income economies, 3.09\% in upper-middle-, 8.73\% in lower-middle- and 28.9\% in low-income economies, a 45-fold difference between the first and last groups. The \$20 tier meets the 2\% benchmark in every high-income economy, in 22\% of upper-middle-income economies and in none of the lower-middle- or low-income economies. The 113 of 201 economies that exceed it are home to 60\% of the working-age population of the economies covered (which account for 98.6\% of the world's), and 45\% of the covered working-age population lives where the burden exceeds 5\%. The \$8 entry tier meets the benchmark in 95\% of upper-middle-income economies but in no lower-middle- or low-income economy. At a \$100 monthly reference fee the median low-income burden exceeds per-capita income itself.

About half of the gradient coincides with connectivity. \paragraph{Changes in relative and absolute dispersion.} Between the first half of 2025 and the second quarter of 2026 the median economy's user share rose by 5.5 points in the high-income group, 3.7 in the upper-middle-, 2.0 in the lower-middle- and 0.9 in the low-income group (Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics}). In percentage points the opposite holds: each log point of income is associated with a 1.37-point larger gain (s.e.\ 0.13), the standard deviation rose from 10.9 to 12.8 points, and the gap between the median high- and lower-middle-income economy widened from 16.3 to 20.3 points. The measured gradient also steepened, though not continuously. On the 111 economies observed in all five windows, the elasticity was 0.68 in August and November 2025, 0.74 in February 2026, 0.77 in April and 0.73 in May 2026. The increase from August 2025 to February 2026 (0.064, clustered s.e.\ 0.018) is significant, whereas the change from February to May 2026 is not ($-0.006$, s.e.\ 0.016). Anthropic's releases changed over this period, with later releases adding Cowork sessions and monthly windows, so the series describes measured Claude use rather than a strictly like-for-like quantity; estimates on all economies meeting Anthropic's thresholds in each window are similar (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:auitime}), and the steepening is in line with Anthropic's own finding that low-adoption countries fell slightly further behind \citep{massenkoff2026learning}. The composition of use differs as well: the share of Claude conversations devoted to coursework falls by 4.7 points per log point of income, from 35\% in the median low-income economy (five economies) to 17\% in the median high-income economy. This gradient is consistent with evidence that learning is a leading entry point in lower-income regions \citep{microsoft2026q2,daepp2026how} and with Anthropic's observation that coursework may be better suited to free use \citep{appel2026primitives}. A third source, Google's per-capita adoption quintile for Gemini, is also positively but less tightly related to income (Spearman correlation 0.46 across 195 economies), although a rank measure and different product coverage prevent direct elasticity comparison.

\paragraph{Direct estimation of the gradient difference.} On a common sample, the difference of the two log-income slopes equals the slope in a regression of $\ln(AUI_c/D_c)$ on log income, with or without an identical set of controls. None of these calculations separates the vendor-share and conversations-per-user components of equation~\eqref{eq:aui_identity}.

\subsection{Localised entry tiers and adoption: the first rollouts of ChatGPT Go}\label{sec:rollout}

The cross-country gradients cannot say whether a cheaper tier changes use. The staggered arrival of ChatGPT Go offers a first look. The tier reached India on 19 August 2025, Indonesia on 22 September, sixteen further Asian economies on 8 October and 71 undisclosed economies on 14 October 2025, then Brazil and eight European economies at the end of that month, and became available everywhere on 16 January 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:data}). India also received twelve months of the tier at no charge from 4 November 2025. Against these dates the public indicators offer four semi-annual or quarterly windows of any use (H1 2025 before any rollout; H2 2025, in which India, Indonesia and the sixteen Asian economies were exposed for 2.8--4.4 months and most others for at most 2.5; Q1 and Q2 2026 with the tier universal) and five windows of Claude activity (4--11 August 2025, before India's launch; 13--20 November 2025; February, April and May 2026). The model of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts no change in any use when a free tier already exists, and a cross-provider response only through substitution.

Because the comparison group was itself partly exposed from mid-October, the H2 2025 contrast is one of exposure length, not of treatment against no treatment, and the design is a case comparison rather than an event study. For each window I regress the change in the log indicator on log income and the initial log level in the economies that did not receive the tier early, compute every economy's residual change from those coefficients, and report the residual of India and the mean residual of the sixteen Asian economies with its rank among all residuals (a placebo-in-space permutation $p$-value) and, for the sixteen, the coefficient on a cohort indicator with an HC3 standard error. The specification was written into the analysis script before any outcome was computed and is reproduced by the replication package; it was not registered externally, and the robustness variants reported below were added after the main results were seen.

Table~\ref{tab:rollout} and Figure~\ref{fig:rollout} show a small, consistent pattern. In the half-year in which the sixteen Asian economies received the tier a week before 71 other economies and two to three months before the rest, their share of adults using any generative-AI product grew 2.5\% faster than income and starting level predict (0.024 log points, s.e.\ 0.010), a difference exceeded by one of 2,000 random groups of comparison economies; ten of the eleven economies have positive residuals. India, with the longest exposure and a free year from November, grew 4.9\% faster than predicted (0.048 log points), a residual exceeded in absolute value by 8\% of economies; Indonesia's residual is small. In the two quarters after the tier became universal the sixteen economies' excess growth falls to 1.0\% and 0.8\% and is no longer distinguishable from chance, which is what a one-off effect of the tier's arrival would produce. On the competing product, Claude activity in the sixteen economies fell 16.5\% relative to others between November 2025 and February 2026 ($-0.180$ log points, s.e.\ 0.063; eleven of fourteen residuals negative), after no significant change in the window ending five weeks after the rollout; India's Claude activity fell 15.3\% relative to others between August and November 2025 ($-0.166$ log points), a residual that 39\% of economies exceed.

Three qualifications matter. The sixteen economies are all in South and South-East Asia, and with region fixed effects the any-use contrast keeps its size (0.021) but doubles its standard error, so it cannot be separated from a regional trend; the Claude contrast likewise loses significance. The rollout order was chosen by the vendor, which released the tier first in markets it expected to grow, and launches came with marketing, so the excess growth could reflect awareness rather than price. And the only pre-period is a single half-year, so parallel trends cannot be checked. Read as a first look, the pattern is compatible with a modest effect of a cheap tier's arrival on whether people use AI at all, in the range of 2--5\% of the user base, and with substitution away from a competing product; it is not compatible with a large extensive-margin response to the price of paid access, and it does not identify the paid margin at all. The free-tier model's strict prediction of no extensive-margin response is therefore at the edge of what the data can reject: a paid option attractive enough to draw directly from non-use (the reversed regime of Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel}) or a launch-awareness effect would both produce what is observed.

\subsection{Policy counterfactuals}\label{sec:policy}

The audit also fixes the magnitudes that pricing and subsidy policies would have to move. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the evidence does and does not show}

The evidence supports four statements. Listed prices for the standard tier are nearly identical across countries, and the only material localisation is of a degraded entry tier, so that the ratio of the frontier price to the entry price rises from 2.5 in the United States to 4 in the median low-income storefront. A uniform dollar price therefore imposes burdens that differ by a factor of about 45 across income groups, a gradient that survives alternative denominators, benchmarks and income vintages. The two public usage indicators differ in their income gradients: any use is moderately income-graded, with about half of its gradient coinciding with connectivity and no decline in the ratio of AI users to internet users as the burden of a paid plan rises, whereas per-capita Claude activity is twice as income-graded and became more so between late 2025 and early 2026. And around the first rollouts of a cheap entry tier, the exposed economies' any-use share grew a few per cent faster than predicted during the exposure window and not afterwards, while activity on a competing product fell relative to others.

The evidence does not establish that prices cause the steeper Claude gradient, nor that the entry tier caused the excess growth. The free-tier model of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts the observed ordering of gradients, but so could several alternatives. Language: frontier assistants perform best in high-resource languages, and English-language interactions are over-represented where local languages are poorly supported \citep{daepp2026how}. Product and brand: Claude's share among AI users may vary with awareness, occupation and competing products, and it is less known in many lower-income markets than ChatGPT, Gemini or Meta's assistant. Work composition: Claude's usage skews toward software and professional tasks, whose share of employment rises with income \citep{gmyrek2025generative}. Measurement: Microsoft's estimator shrinks noisy countries toward the global average and floors device-scaling factors, which compresses cross-country variation, while Anthropic's publication thresholds select countries on conversation volume, and the index pools free and paid accounts, so conversations per user may reflect work practices rather than plan status. The robustness checks address some of these concerns, since the gap persists without high-income economies and conditional on education, and the rollout comparison adds variation in prices that is not a function of income, but that variation is a few weeks of exposure across four windows in economies chosen by the vendor. The India case illustrates both the promise and the limits of such events: within three months in late 2025, three frontier tiers became free to Indian subscribers of Airtel (Perplexity Pro, from July), Jio (Google AI Pro, from October) and OpenAI (ChatGPT Go, from November); app-analytics firms recorded a ninefold rise in Perplexity's downloads while ChatGPT's and Claude's stayed flat \citep{techcrunch2026perplexity}, and India's any-use share grew about 5\% faster than predicted (Table~\ref{tab:rollout}), which shows that product-level demand responds strongly to a zero price while aggregate use responds much less.

\subsection{From conditional gains to convergence}\label{sec:equal}

Suppose that AI raises output in economy $i$ by $\Delta Y_i/Y_i = s_L E_i a_i g_i$, where $s_L$ is the labour share, $E_i$ the share of labour tasks exposed to AI, $a_i$ the adoption rate in exposed tasks and $g_i$ the task-level productivity gain, as in Hulten-style accounting applied to AI \citep{hulten1978growth,acemoglu2019automation,acemoglu2025simple}. For two economies (or groups) $H$ and $L$ with output per worker $y_H>y_L$, the relative gap moves from $R_0=y_H/y_L$ to
\begin{equation}
\frac{R_1}{R_0} = \frac{1 + s_L E_H a_H g_H}{1 + s_L E_L a_L g_L}.
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Access-conditional equalisation]\label{prop:equal}
The relative gap narrows if and only if $E_L a_L g_L > E_H a_H g_H$. Equivalently, the adoption rate in $L$ needed to avoid divergence is
\[
a_L^{*} = a_H \cdot \frac{E_H}{E_L}\cdot \frac{g_H}{g_L}.
\]
Larger conditional gains for the less productive ($g_L > g_H$) are neither necessary nor sufficient for convergence.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:ratio}, $R_1<R_0$ if and only if $E_La_Lg_L>E_Ha_Hg_H$; solving the equality for $a_L$ gives $a_L^*$. If $g_L>g_H$ but $E_La_L<E_Ha_Hg_H/g_L$ the gap widens, so $g_L>g_H$ is not sufficient; if $g_L\le g_H$ but $E_La_L$ is large enough the gap narrows, so it is not necessary.
\end{proof}

The proposition separates two statements that are often conflated. ``AI helps the less skilled more'' is a statement about $g$ conditional on use, whereas ``AI narrows gaps'' also depends on exposure $E$ and adoption $a$, both of which rise with income. Evidence on $g$ is itself mixed outside the settings cited above: in a randomised trial with Kenyan entrepreneurs, access to a GPT-4 business mentor raised the performance of initially strong performers by over 15\% and lowered that of weak performers by nearly 10\% \citep{otis2025uneven}. An illustration shows the orders of magnitude, with the caveat that the available proxies have different denominators. The share of employment in occupations exposed to generative AI is 34\% in high-income and 11\% in low-income economies \citep{gmyrek2025generative}, and the shares of the working-age population using any generative-AI product are about 32\% and 9\% (Section~\ref{sec:extensive}; the latter includes region-imputed values). Occupational exposure is not task exposure and population-wide use is not within-task adoption, so these figures only stand in for $E$ and $a$. On them, $a_L^*$ would be 1.00 if task-level gains were equal, 0.67 if they were 1.5 times larger in $L$ and 0.50 if twice as large; the exposure gap alone implies that, at equal adoption, task-level gains in $L$ would have to be more than three times those in $H$. The point is qualitative: conditional equalisation does not aggregate into convergence unless access and exposure keep pace, and on the available proxies they do not. I refer to the possibility that AI equalises conditional on use yet widens gaps unconditionally as the \emph{AI access paradox}. The rollout evidence of Section~\ref{sec:rollout} bears on $a$ only at the entry tier: a cheap tier moves use modestly, and the margin that would have to move for convergence, paid access to frontier capability, is the one that remains priced globally.

\subsection{A feasible evaluation agenda}

Such variation now exists, and Section~\ref{sec:rollout} used the first of it. ChatGPT Go was rolled out to India and then to 170 further countries on a staggered schedule between August 2025 and January 2026 \citep{openai2026go}; Google introduced and repriced AI Plus and AI Ultra in 2025--2026 \citep{google2026io}; OpenAI changed the allowances and eligibility of its Pro tiers in September 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:tariff}); several markets saw temporary premium tiers bundled with mobile subscriptions; and App Store price schedules change with exchange rates and taxes. Combined with the quarterly Microsoft series and the monthly Anthropic series, and with higher-frequency outcomes such as search interest or app installs, these events support difference-in-differences and event-study designs that compare economies before and after a localised tier becomes available, with the pre-treatment observations that Section~\ref{sec:rollout} lacked.

The requirements are demanding. Outcomes must be measured at the level of the intervention: effective prices, paid conversion, active users, use per user and cancellations for the provider and plan concerned, on stable population bases and product coverage. The timing of tier introductions may respond to market growth and coincide with marketing, bundles or model upgrades, so an event study needs sufficient pre-treatment observations, plausible comparison groups, checks for anticipation and spillovers, and estimators that accommodate heterogeneous effects across rollout cohorts \citep{callaway2021did}. A response of Claude usage to a ChatGPT price change measures a cross-provider effect, not ChatGPT's own-price elasticity of paid adoption, and currency or tax changes also move incomes and other purchases, so they are not automatically valid instruments. Randomised discounts or institutional-access offers, with free-tier substitution and employer-provided access recorded separately, would identify effects on take-up most cleanly. Vendors could make all of these designs far more precise by publishing country-level paid-subscription counts and effective prices.

\subsection{Policy implications}

The results bear on four policy questions, ordered by the margin they concern.

\emph{Connectivity as a precondition, for the extensive margin.} Connection, devices and electricity are preconditions for any use, and half of the use gradient coincides with the connectivity gradient. The flat ratio of AI users to internet users below the high-income group is consistent with the view that access and complements, rather than price, limit whether the connected use AI at all \citep{worldbank2026wdr,itu2025ff}, although it does not demonstrate this, and compute and data infrastructure add a further layer \citep{lehdonvirta2024compute}. Evidence from fast internet in Africa suggests that such investments raise employment and productivity in their own right \citep{hjort2019arrival}.

\emph{Localise frontier access, not only degraded tiers, for the paid margin.} The welfare effects of third-degree price discrimination are ambiguous in general, but they tend to be positive when discrimination opens markets that uniform pricing would leave unserved \citep{varian1985price,aguirre2010monopoly}, as may be the case for frontier tiers whose list price exceeds a quarter of average income. Local-currency storefronts, carrier billing and time- or usage-limited frontier access at locally indexed prices are practical steps; if cross-border arbitrage is what keeps the \$20 tier uniform, usage caps can contain it without withholding frontier models. The counterfactuals of Section~\ref{sec:policy} fix the magnitudes: entry-tier localisation applied to the frontier tier closes about half of the gap to the benchmark in poorer economies, whereas a subsidy or institutional purchase that closes it costs a few dollars per person per week. Whether a lower-allowance tier suffices for a given task is not something the audit can settle.

\emph{Institutional access.} Evidence on AI's productivity effects comes overwhelmingly from employer-provided tools rather than individual subscriptions \citep{brynjolfsson2025generative,cui2024effects}. Schools, universities, public agencies and employers can decouple access from household income; a World Bank trial in Nigeria found sizeable learning gains from a supervised GPT-4 tutoring programme \citep{desimone2025chalkboards}. Research on subsidies for new health products suggests that free trials can raise long-run adoption through learning rather than crowding it out \citep{dupas2014short}, while even small prices can sharply reduce take-up among the poor \citep{cohen2010free}. The Indian bundles of 2025 are, in effect, a large uncontrolled trial of free frontier access; whether their users keep paying when the free year ends is the first thing to measure.

\emph{Transparency.} Research on AI access is constrained by the absence of public data on paid subscriptions, effective prices and usage limits by country. Machine-readable disclosure of dated country-level prices, taxes, supported payment methods, plan limits and product definitions, and usage statistics that distinguish free from paid accounts and users from use per user, would allow the designs above to be implemented. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} lists the fields such a record should contain.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

First, burdens are normalised prices rather than expenditures, and quintile incomes are approximations based on survey shares that mix income and consumption concepts; a person living in an economy with a high national-average ratio is not necessarily unable to afford a subscription. Second, the App Store audit observes listed prices in one channel on one day. Listed prices can differ from web prices and from amounts charged at checkout, they include platform commissions and taxes that the regressions do not separate, and the archive stores parsed listing records rather than the original pages, so the monthly billing period of each listing is inferred from names and price magnitudes rather than confirmed from product metadata. Promotions and bundles are not captured. Third, the Microsoft series is a modelled estimate built on desktop telemetry, and country-specific values are unavailable for most low-income economies; results for that group rest on three economies or on regional pools, and the two-indicator comparison includes a single low-income economy. Fourth, the Anthropic index covers one vendor's consumer product and does not distinguish paid from free users, so it proxies the paid margin imperfectly, and its coverage changed across releases. Fifth, the cross-country regressions are descriptive, and their standard errors omit the uncertainty inside the providers' estimators. Sixth, the rollout comparison observes exposure differences of a few weeks across four or five windows, with a comparison group that was itself partly exposed, no pre-trend check, a rollout order chosen by the vendor and a design that was fixed in the script but not registered before the outcomes were computed; it cannot separate the price of the tier from the attention its launch attracted. Seventh, the framework holds preferences, dispersion and task value fixed, omits competing providers, employer access and welfare, and is not calibrated to the usage indicators; the convergence arithmetic uses occupational exposure and population-wide use as proxies for task exposure and within-task adoption.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Generative AI is priced as a global product and experienced as a local one. The audit in this paper shows that the vendors of the leading assistants localise only the bottom of their menus. A capability-limited entry tier costs about \$5 a month in most poorer markets, but the \$20 standard plan and the \$100--200 high-usage plans are listed at almost the same price everywhere, so the frontier plan costs two and a half times the entry plan in the United States and four times in the median low-income economy. The standard plan accordingly costs less than 1\% of average income in the median rich country and more than a quarter in the median poor one, fails the broadband affordability benchmark for most of the world's working-age population and, in low-income economies, exceeds it even at the synthetic mean resources of the richest fifth of households.

Two pieces of evidence bear on what this pricing does. Across countries, the share of adults using any generative-AI product is only moderately related to income, and half of that relation coincides with internet access, whereas per-capita activity on one frontier assistant is twice as strongly related to income; this contrast is what a free tier that separates trying AI from paying for it would produce, but it is also what differences in language, product reach and measurement would produce. Around the first rollouts of ChatGPT Go, the economies that received the cheap tier early saw use grow a few per cent faster than expected, and only while many others still lacked the tier; the effect is small, confined to the entry tier and not distinguishable from regional trends. Neither piece measures paid conversion or frontier use directly.

For policy the message is where to look. Cheap entry tiers already exist and, on this evidence, move use at most modestly. Paid access to frontier capability is what remains priced globally, and the counterfactuals show that it is also the margin that policy can move: localising it as the entry tier is localised would close only about half of the gap to the benchmark in poorer economies, while bringing it to the benchmark through subsidies or institutional licences costs a few dollars per person per week, or 0.1--1.5\% of GNI for a tenth of the working-age population. Whether access to frontier capability keeps pace with its diffusion in rich countries will depend more on who can obtain it, for how much use and how soon after release, than on how capable the models become. Measuring that will require what no vendor yet publishes: paid subscriptions, effective prices and entitlements by country.

\section{A conditional framework for free and paid access}\label{app:tiermodel}
\label{sec:conditional_access}

Consider a connected individual choosing between no use, a free tier and a paid
tier. Let $v>0$ denote the individual's monthly monetary valuation of one unit
of effective AI service. For example, $v=\theta w$, where $w$ is monthly income
and $\theta$ captures the relevance of AI to the individual's activities. Let
$0<q_F<q_P$ describe the tiers' service levels, $p_c>0$ the monthly paid price,
and $\tau>0$ a monthly monetary equivalent of the time, learning and other
participation costs common to both tiers. Utilities are
\begin{equation}
 u_0=0,\qquad u_F=vq_F-\tau,\qquad u_P=vq_P-\tau-p_c.
 \label{eq:tier_utilities}
\end{equation}
These service levels are theoretical objects; they need not correspond to
particular models, observed subscription labels or a measured technological
frontier. The framework omits competing suppliers, employer-provided access,
sharing and usage-dependent participation costs.

Write $\Delta q=q_P-q_F$. Direct comparison of all three options gives the
thresholds for any use and paid use:
\begin{equation}
 T_{D,c}=\min\left\{\frac{\tau}{q_F},\frac{\tau+p_c}{q_P}\right\},
 \qquad
 T_{P,c}=\max\left\{\frac{p_c}{\Delta q},\frac{\tau+p_c}{q_P}\right\}.
 \label{eq:general_thresholds}
\end{equation}
Thus a free option does not, by itself, make aggregate adoption independent of
the paid price. A sufficiently attractive paid option can draw individuals
directly from non-use.

Let $\kappa_c$ be the population share with the connectivity and complementary
resources needed to participate. Conditional on belonging to this group, assume
\begin{equation}
 \ln v\sim N(\mu_c,s^2),\qquad
 \mu_c=\mu_0+\ln(y_c/y_0),\qquad s>0,
 \label{eq:conditional_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $y_c$ is a country income index and $y_0$ a reference value. This
location-shift assumption is a stylisation, not an identity linking national
GNI to household incomes or returns to AI. Defining the distribution conditional
on eligibility makes the multiplication by $\kappa_c$ explicit; applying the
same distribution unconditionally would require an additional selection
assumption. Holding qualities, participation costs and dispersion common,
the population shares using any tier and the paid tier are
\begin{equation}
 D_c=\kappa_c\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_c-\ln T_{D,c}}{s}\right),\qquad
 P_c=\kappa_c\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_c-\ln T_{P,c}}{s}\right).
 \label{eq:general_shares}
\end{equation}
The assumption of a common $\tau$ is substantive. If participation costs vary as $\tau_c\propto y_c^\lambda$, the nested-regime adoption slope becomes $\varepsilon_\kappa+(1-\lambda)h(z_D)/s$ even at a fixed free service level. Monetary time costs can rise with wages; the baseline assumption therefore rules out an economically relevant source of slope variation. Arbitrary heterogeneity in
participation costs cannot generally be absorbed into $v$ while retaining both
threshold formulas and a common paid price.

\paragraph{Threshold ordering.}
If
\begin{equation}
 \frac{p_c}{\Delta q}>\frac{\tau}{q_F},
 \label{eq:nested_condition}
\end{equation}
then $T_{D,c}=\tau/q_F$ and $T_{P,c}=p_c/\Delta q$.
Within this regime, a marginal paid-price change leaves $D_c$ unchanged,
provided $\kappa_c$, qualities and participation costs do not change. If the
inequality is reversed, both thresholds equal $(\tau+p_c)/q_P$ and paid price
affects any use as well as paid use.

\paragraph{Proof.}
The free tier is preferable to non-use when $v\geq\tau/q_F$. The paid tier is
preferable to non-use when $v\geq(\tau+p_c)/q_P$, and preferable to the free tier
when $v\geq p_c/\Delta q$. These comparisons establish
equation~\eqref{eq:general_thresholds}. Under
condition~\eqref{eq:nested_condition}, the three thresholds are ordered as
$\tau/q_F<(\tau+p_c)/q_P<p_c/\Delta q$. Reversing the condition reverses this
ordering, establishing both regimes. At equality the thresholds coincide.
\qed

\begin{aproposition}[Local income gradients]\label{prop:margins}
Within the strict nested-threshold regime, define
$z_D=(\mu_c-\ln(\tau/q_F))/s$,
$z_P=(\mu_c-\ln(p_c/\Delta q))/s$,
$h(z)=\phi(z)/\Phi(z)$,
$\beta=\partial\ln p_c/\partial\ln y_c$, and
$\varepsilon_\kappa=\partial\ln\kappa_c/\partial\ln y_c$.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_D=\varepsilon_\kappa+\frac{h(z_D)}{s},\qquad
 \varepsilon_P=\varepsilon_\kappa+(1-\beta)\frac{h(z_P)}{s}.
 \label{eq:income_gradients}
\end{equation}
Uniform pricing gives $\varepsilon_P>\varepsilon_D$. Holding the local price
level and hence the threshold values fixed, this ordering persists if
$\beta<\beta^*\equiv1-h(z_D)/h(z_P)$, where $0<\beta^*<1$.\end{aproposition}

\paragraph{Proof.}
Differentiating the logarithms of equation~\eqref{eq:general_shares} yields
equation~\eqref{eq:income_gradients}, since
$\partial\mu_c/\partial\ln y_c=1$. The strict threshold ordering implies
$z_P<z_D$. The lower-tail inverse Mills ratio $h$ is strictly decreasing, so
$h(z_P)>h(z_D)$. Subtracting the elasticities and solving for their equality
gives $\beta^*$. \qed

The threshold $\beta^*$ depends on how far the two margins sit in the tail of the valuation distribution. Writing $D_c/\kappa_c=\Phi(z_D)$ for the share of eligible individuals who use any tier and $\pi$ for the share of users who pay, so that $\Phi(z_P)=\pi\Phi(z_D)$, gives $\beta^*=0.44$ when 23\% of the eligible use AI and 10\% of users pay, $0.34$ when a quarter pay, and $0.58$ when 1\% pay; $\beta^*$ falls below the observed standard-tier slope of 0.04 only if more than about nine in ten users pay. With the paid share of users unobserved, the condition is plausible but untested.

The comparison across $\beta$ is local. One implementation compares schedules
$p_\beta(y)=p_0(y/y_0)^\beta$ at their common anchor $y_0$. Away from that anchor,
changing the pricing schedule also moves the paid threshold; the proposition
does not establish a global effect of a localisation policy. Nor does it
establish welfare effects. Its substantive implication is conditional: with
nested thresholds and the maintained distributional assumptions, a free tier
can separate the determinants of initial adoption from those of upgrading.

In the reversed-threshold regime, the same calculation instead gives
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_D=\varepsilon_P
 =\varepsilon_\kappa+
 \left(1-\beta\frac{p_c}{\tau+p_c}\right)\frac{h(z)}{s},
 \qquad z=\frac{\mu_c-\ln((\tau+p_c)/q_P)}{s}.
 \label{eq:reversed_gradients}
\end{equation}
There is then no interval in which the free tier is the individual's optimal
choice. This limiting case makes clear why the empirical presence of free
products alone cannot establish price independence. More generally, differences
across countries in language support, time costs, service quality, selection
into connectivity or the dispersion of valuations introduce additional terms
into the gradients. These possibilities are substantively relevant and are not
ruled out by an income regression. The framework should therefore organise
questions about access while leaving these competing explanations open.

\section{Connectivity accounting}\label{app:connectivity}

Define the ratio $R_c=D_c/I_c$, where $D_c$ is Microsoft's working-age AI-use share and $I_c$ is the all-age internet-use share, so that $\ln D_c=\ln I_c+\ln R_c$ exactly. In the 109-country estimation sample no ratio exceeds one. The slopes on log income are 0.182 for internet use and 0.189 for the ratio, summing to the adoption slope of 0.370 up to rounding, so the connectivity component accounts for 49\% of the gradient. This is an accounting decomposition: the denominator has a different population base and often a different year, internet penetration already enters Microsoft's estimator, and holding income fixed the elasticity of AI use with respect to internet use is 0.05 (s.e.\ 0.20), far from the value of one that a pure connectivity channel would imply.

Across groups (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}, Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}) the connectivity share of the gap to high-income economies rises from 21\% for upper-middle-income to 49\% for lower-middle-income and 67\% for the three low-income economies with country-specific data. The ratio of AI users to internet users is 22.7\%, 23.6\% and 23.0\% from the upper-middle- to the low-income group, against 37.5\% in high-income economies, and its income slope across non-high-income economies is $-0.03$ (s.e.\ 0.06). The comparison rests on group means, the low-income row contains only three economies, and the ratio mixes age bases; it is not a directly observed probability of adopting AI conditional on being online.

\section{A protocol for recording access conditions}\label{app:protocol}

A recurring fee does not determine the cost of completing useful work. A reproducible observation of access conditions should record the country, observation time, billing channel, provider, plan and billing period; the original currency and tax convention; listing and checkout status; subscriber cohort; included allowance and reset rules; model and tool eligibility; and overage terms. Missing terms should remain missing rather than be assigned a provider-wide constant. The replication package supplies a field dictionary for these observations (\nolinkurl{checks/access_measurement_fields.csv}). It is a proposed extension of the present audit, not a newly collected dataset.

For a prespecified workload $B$, quality rule $q$ and deadline $d$, define the feasible set $\mathcal F(B,q,d;c,t)$ of service configurations that actually meet all three requirements in country $c$ at time $t$. The minimum cash expenditure is
\[
C(B,q,d;c,t)=\inf_{a\in\mathcal F(B,q,d;c,t)}\operatorname{cashcost}(a).
\]
The comparison must state whether free, API, institutional and local-model configurations are included and how fixed hardware or subscription costs are allocated. Feasibility requires task-level observations of correctness, language, model version, wall-clock completion time, retries, billed use and failures. This paper does not observe those quantities and does not estimate $C$. A prospective evaluation should report total expenditure and success rates alongside cost per successful task, since excluding failed attempts would understate costs. The protocol distinguishes measured subscription commitment from a future quality- and deadline-constrained access outcome.
