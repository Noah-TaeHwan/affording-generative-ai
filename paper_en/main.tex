\begin{abstract}
\noindent Consumer generative AI is sold on a subscription menu that is nearly identical across countries, while national incomes differ by two orders of magnitude. This paper measures how far local prices adjust to that difference. I audit the tax-inclusive local prices listed for nine plans of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini in 170 national Apple App Store storefronts on a single day, and compute the annual cost of each plan as a share of GNI per capita for 201 economies. Standard \$20 plans are priced almost uniformly: the income slope of the local dollar price is 0.03--0.04, against 0.23 if prices tracked local price levels. Only OpenAI's entry tier is partly localised (slope 0.14), and every low-income storefront prices in U.S. dollars. A \$20 plan therefore costs 0.65\% of per-capita income a year in the median high-income economy and 29\% in the median low-income economy, and 60\% of the working-age population of the covered economies lives where it exceeds the 2\% benchmark used for broadband. Two public usage indicators differ in their income gradients, 0.37 for any generative-AI use and 0.71 for per-capita Claude activity, a contrast consistent with, though not a test of, a model in which free tiers detach initial adoption from the paid price. Larger productivity gains for less-skilled users, moreover, do not imply convergence across countries unless exposure and adoption keep pace. The audit is a one-day snapshot of listed prices; the paper sets out what the evaluation of pricing and access policies would require.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} generative AI; affordability; digital divide; international price discrimination; technology diffusion; development.\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} O33, O15, L11, L86, D63, F63.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Field and experimental evidence suggests that generative AI raises productivity, with the largest gains often accruing to less-experienced workers \citep{brynjolfsson2025generative,noy2023experimental,dellacqua2023navigating,peng2023impact}. Those findings concern people who already had access. Whether the same opportunity exists across countries depends first on whether people can use the tools, and frontier capability is sold to individuals as a subscription on a menu that is standardised worldwide: a free tier, a standard tier at \$20 a month and high-usage tiers at about \$100 and \$200 (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The same dollar price weighs very differently on different incomes. Twenty dollars a month is 0.3\% of per-capita income in the United States and more than 40\% in Mozambique.

This paper asks how far local subscription prices adjust to differences in national income, and what a uniform price implies for the affordability of generative AI across countries. Its contribution is a dated, multi-provider audit of local prices: the tax-inclusive prices listed for nine plans of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini in every Apple App Store storefront (218 codes, 170 with local price lists), collected on 27 September 2026 through a single payment channel, together with the annual cost of each plan as a share of GNI per capita for 201 economies. Earlier work compared ChatGPT Plus's \$20 fee with national incomes \citep{sathish2024llempower}; this audit adds local rather than U.S. prices, several providers and tiers, and a record of retrieval outcomes, currencies and plan matching. A matched comparison of ChatGPT Go and Plus within each storefront checks that the entry-tier result does not depend on currency conversion.

Four findings follow. \emph{Standard plans are priced almost uniformly.} Across storefronts the income slope of the local dollar price is 0.039 for ChatGPT Plus, 0.026 for Claude Pro and 0.037 for Google AI Pro, against 0.23 if prices tracked local price levels and 1 under proportional pricing. \emph{Only OpenAI's entry tier is partly localised.} ChatGPT Go's slope is 0.143, about 60\% of the price-level slope and one-seventh of proportional pricing; Google's entry tier is listed at \$4.99 in every non-high-income group. Every storefront in a low-income economy prices in U.S. dollars, and 36\% of low-income economies have no local storefront at all. \emph{Burdens are therefore steeply income-graded.} A \$20 plan costs 0.65\% of per-capita income a year in the median high-income economy, 3.1\% in upper-middle-, 8.7\% in lower-middle- and 29\% in low-income economies. It meets the 2\% benchmark that the ITU and the UN Broadband Commission apply to broadband only where per-capita income is at least \$12,000, and 60\% of the working-age population of the covered economies lives where it does not. For the richest fifth of households in the median low-income economy, one plan would still cost 13\% of average income. \emph{Public usage indicators differ in their income gradients.} Microsoft's estimate of the share of adults using any generative-AI product has an income elasticity of 0.37, about half of which coincides with the gradient in internet use; Anthropic's index of per-capita Claude use has an elasticity of 0.71 on the same 96 economies, of which only one is low-income.

A simple framework organises these facts. In a three-option model of non-use, free use and paid use, a free tier detaches the decision to use AI at all from the paid price whenever the upgrade threshold lies above the free-adoption threshold; the paid margin then inherits the full income gradient unless prices are localised (Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel}). The steeper gradient of Claude activity is consistent with this mechanism, but cross-country data cannot identify it: vendor choice, conversations per user and measurement processes can produce the same ordering, and the index pools free and paid accounts. A second result concerns convergence. Because aggregate gains depend on exposure and adoption as well as on task-level gains, larger conditional gains for less-skilled users are neither necessary nor sufficient for AI to narrow gaps between countries (Proposition~\ref{prop:equal}). On rough proxies for exposure and adoption, low-income adoption would have to reach about 100\% for equal task-level gains to narrow the gap, and 50--67\% even if gains there were 1.5--2 times larger, against about 9\% today.

The paper relates to several literatures. Studies of who adopts generative AI within countries find adoption skewed toward the young, the educated and higher earners \citep{bick2024rapid,humlum2025unequal}; cross-country studies based on web traffic, telemetry or conversation data document large gaps by income \citep{liu2024who,liu2025who,misra2025measuring,chatterji2025how,appel2025uneven,daepp2026how,iscenko2026atlas}; and direct surveys are beginning to distinguish free from paid use \citep{kanzamanova2026digital}. Earlier work on computers, the internet and mobile telephony identified income, human capital and prices as the main correlates of diffusion \citep{caselli2001cross,chinn2007determinants,aker2010mobile,bjorkegren2019adoption}, and the diffusion literature has long distinguished the timing of adoption from the intensity of use \citep{comin2004cross,comin2010exploration}: \citet{comin2018technology} show that for earlier technologies adoption lags converged across countries while usage intensity diverged. Studies of occupational exposure to AI \citep{felten2021occupational,eloundou2024gpts,cazzaniga2024genai,gmyrek2025generative} supply the exposure figures used below, and development-oriented assessments \citep{korinek2021artificial,undp2025hdr,unctad2025tir,worldbank2026wdr} frame the policy stakes. The affordability convention for telecommunications \citep{itu2025ff} supplies the benchmark extended here, and the literature on international pricing and product versioning \citep{goldberg1997goods,cavallo2014currency,mussa1978monopoly,goldfarb2019digital} frames the audit, although taxes, platform commissions and product differences mean that the estimated slopes are not firms' price-discrimination parameters.

The analysis is descriptive. Because a uniform dollar price makes the burden a monotone transformation of income, a cross-country association between burdens and use cannot identify a price effect; the audit also postdates the usage windows and observes one channel on one day. The staggered rollout of localised entry tiers, and the tariff change that OpenAI announced two days after the audit, now make evaluation designs feasible, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} sets out what they require.

Section~\ref{sec:background} describes the price menu and the subsequent tariff change. Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the burden measure and presents the framework. Section~\ref{sec:data} documents the data, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the audit, the burdens and the usage comparison, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses interpretation, evaluation designs, policy and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. The formal model, data construction, a connectivity decomposition, additional tables, a \$500 reference-fee scenario and a measurement protocol are in the appendices.

\emph{Convergence.} The three menus have converged on nearly the same rungs: a free tier, an entry tier at \$5--8, a \$20 standard tier, and tiers at about \$100 and \$200. Using several assistants multiplies the cost: a professional who keeps the \$20 tier of all three pays about \$60 a month.

\emph{Quantity, not intelligence.} The upper rungs are differentiated primarily by usage allowances (``5$\times$'' and ``20$\times$'' the standard tier) and, secondarily, by access to the most capable model variants and features. With near-zero marginal cost of replication but non-trivial inference costs, digital goods lend themselves to versioning \citep{goldfarb2019digital}, and the menu is a textbook instance of second-degree price discrimination by quantity and quality \citep{mussa1978monopoly}. The free and entry tiers are degraded versions of the standard tier: ChatGPT Go, for instance, offers an ``Instant'' model with lower caps, while Plus offers the ``Thinking'' model with higher limits \citep{openai2026go}. A higher price therefore buys more frontier capability per month, although neither the plan name nor the price is a comparable measure of useful capability across vendors, languages or tasks.

\emph{Localisation happens at the bottom of the ladder.} The entry tiers are the vendors' instrument for reaching lower-income markets: ChatGPT Go launched in India in August 2025 and was rolled out to 170 further countries before its U.S. launch at \$8 in January 2026 \citep{openai2026go}. Section~\ref{sec:prices} shows that it is the only rung whose local price varies materially with income. Promotional give-aways, such as bundles of premium plans with mobile subscriptions in India, further lower effective prices in some markets, but they are temporary and country-specific and are not captured by a listing audit.

\subsection{The tariff change of 29 September 2026}\label{sec:tariff}

Two days after the audit, OpenAI announced a Pro 500 tier at \$500 a month, sold initially on the web and including its fastest service mode, and reopened Pro 200 subscriptions with a lower included allowance for new subscribers, while existing subscribers keep their allowance until 29 October 2026 \citep{openai2026releasenotes,openai2026prorevision}. The episode illustrates two measurement points. An unchanged fee can correspond to different entitlements by account cohort and date, and a new tier can add eligibility rights rather than replace a price. A tariff is therefore a bundle $(F,\mathbf{A},\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{w})$ of fee, included allowances, eligible models and service modes, overage terms and reset rules, of which the burden defined below uses the fee $F$ alone. Quality-adjusted prices in the model market fall rapidly \citep{andre2025markets}; they are a different object from the recurring cash commitment of a subscription, and neither identifies the cash cost of completing a given task. The change postdates both the price audit and the usage windows, so it is treated separately: Appendix~\ref{app:500} reports a \$500 reference-fee scenario, and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} sets out a protocol for recording the elements of a tariff that the fee alone omits.

\section{Framework}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The affordability burden}

\begin{definition}[AI affordability burden]
For a plan with monthly price $p_c$ in country $c$ (current U.S. dollars) and per-capita income $y_c$ (current U.S. dollars),
\begin{equation}
\AAB_c(p_c) = \frac{12\,p_c}{y_c}\times 100 .
\label{eq:aab}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The burden is a normalised price: it states how large the price is relative to average income. It is not a budget share and does not measure what anyone spends. The denominator is Atlas-method GNI per capita, which smooths exchange rates over three years. The measure follows the convention of the ITU's broadband affordability statistics, which express the monthly price of an entry-level basket as a share of monthly GNI per capita and assess it against the 2\% benchmark of the UN Broadband Commission \citep{itu2025ff}. I use that benchmark as a borrowed reference and report sensitivity to other cutoffs. For a cutoff $t$ in percentage points the threshold income is $y_c\geq1200p/t$; at 2\% this is \$12,000 for a \$20 plan and \$4,800 for an \$8 plan.

Because average income conceals distribution, I also compute burdens at quintile means, approximating the mean income of quintile $q$ by $y_{cq}=y_c s_{cq}/0.2$, where $s_{cq}$ is the quintile's survey-based income or consumption share. These are synthetic resource levels rather than measured disposable incomes, since survey welfare concepts, survey dates and national accounts differ. GNI also includes income that does not accrue to households, so burdens relative to household final consumption per capita are reported as well.

\begin{remark}[What a PPP denominator measures]\label{rem:ppp}
For a service priced in dollars, the burden relative to income at market exchange rates is the relevant cash-cost quantity. Dividing the dollar price by income at purchasing-power parity instead yields $12p/y^{PPP}_c = 12\,(p\cdot PLR_c)/y_c$, where $PLR_c = y_c/y^{PPP}_c$, the ratio of Atlas to PPP income, is a proxy for the price level relative to the United States.\footnote{Because the Atlas method averages exchange rates over three years, the ratio differs from the same-year GDP price-level ratio by a median of 3.1\% (90th percentile 8.3\%) across 197 economies. Its income elasticity is 0.228, against 0.224 for the GDP price-level ratio.} The same algebra equals the Atlas-income burden under a constructed price $p_c=p\cdot PLR_c$, that is, the burden that would obtain if the vendor indexed its price to local price levels. Because price levels rise with income \citep{balassa1964purchasing,samuelson1964theoretical}, and PPP conversion factors are designed for broad consumption baskets rather than internationally priced digital services \citep{deaton2010understanding}, the PPP-based ratio is best read as this counterfactual pricing scenario rather than as a robustness check on the same quantity. Section~\ref{sec:prices} compares actual pricing with it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A free tier and two margins of access}\label{sec:mechanism}

A free tier permits initial use while a paid tier offers more capability. In the three-option model of Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel}, connected individuals with heterogeneous valuations choose non-use, free use or paid use. When the threshold for upgrading lies above the threshold for using the free tier, the decision to use AI at all is governed by non-monetary hurdles (connectivity, skills, language) and the paid price drops out of it, whereas paying for more capability depends on the ratio of price to income. Proposition~\ref{prop:margins} in Appendix~\ref{app:tiermodel} then predicts a steeper income gradient on the paid margin than on the margin of any use whenever prices are not localised, and the difference falls as localisation increases. If the paid option is attractive enough to draw users directly from non-use, the paid price affects both margins. The model conditions on common participation costs and service levels; it organises the evidence and generates predictions, but the public usage data do not observe paid conversion, so it is not fitted or tested below.

\subsection{Conditional gains and unconditional gaps}\label{sec:equal}

Suppose that AI raises output in economy $i$ by $\Delta Y_i/Y_i = s_L E_i a_i g_i$, where $s_L$ is the labour share, $E_i$ the share of labour tasks exposed to AI, $a_i$ the adoption rate in exposed tasks and $g_i$ the task-level productivity gain, as in Hulten-style accounting applied to AI \citep{hulten1978growth,acemoglu2019automation,acemoglu2025simple}. For two economies (or groups) $H$ and $L$ with output per worker $y_H>y_L$, the relative gap moves from $R_0=y_H/y_L$ to
\begin{equation}
\frac{R_1}{R_0} = \frac{1 + s_L E_H a_H g_H}{1 + s_L E_L a_L g_L}.
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Access-conditional equalisation]\label{prop:equal}
The relative gap narrows if and only if $E_L a_L g_L > E_H a_H g_H$. Equivalently, the adoption rate in $L$ needed to avoid divergence is
\[
a_L^{*} = a_H \cdot \frac{E_H}{E_L}\cdot \frac{g_H}{g_L}.
\]
Larger conditional gains for the less productive ($g_L > g_H$) are neither necessary nor sufficient for convergence.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:ratio}, $R_1<R_0$ if and only if $E_La_Lg_L>E_Ha_Hg_H$; solving the equality for $a_L$ gives $a_L^*$. If $g_L>g_H$ but $E_La_L<E_Ha_Hg_H/g_L$ the gap widens, so $g_L>g_H$ is not sufficient; if $g_L\le g_H$ but $E_La_L$ is large enough the gap narrows, so it is not necessary.
\end{proof}

The proposition separates two statements that are often conflated. ``AI helps the less skilled more'' is a statement about $g$ conditional on use, whereas ``AI narrows gaps'' also depends on exposure $E$ and adoption $a$, both of which rise with income. Evidence on $g$ is itself mixed outside the settings cited above: in a randomised trial with Kenyan entrepreneurs, access to a GPT-4 business mentor raised the performance of initially strong performers by over 15\% and lowered that of weak performers by nearly 10\% \citep{otis2025uneven}. Rough proxies indicate the magnitudes involved. The share of employment in occupations exposed to generative AI is 34\% in high-income and 11\% in low-income economies \citep{gmyrek2025generative}, and the shares of the working-age population using any generative-AI product are about 32\% and 9\% (Section~\ref{sec:extensive}; the latter includes region-imputed values). With these figures standing in for task exposure and within-task adoption, $a_L^*$ is 1.00 if task-level gains are equal, 0.67 if they are 1.5 times larger in $L$ and 0.50 if twice as large. The calculation gives orders of magnitude, not estimates. Its point is that conditional equalisation does not aggregate into convergence unless access and exposure keep pace; I refer to the possibility that AI equalises conditional on use yet widens gaps unconditionally as the \emph{AI access paradox}.

\subsection{What the public usage indicators identify}
\label{sec:usage_measurement}

The paid-adoption share of the model is not observed in the public data. Microsoft's AI User Share is an estimated adoption rate for a set of tracked products. Anthropic's AI Usage Index is a population-normalised measure of Claude conversation activity that pools free and paid accounts; it does not identify the number of paid users or their usage separately. The empirical comparison therefore concerns two observed diffusion indicators, not separately measured extensive and paid margins.

An accounting identity shows the distinction. In a common period and population,
let $N_c$ denote population, $U_c$ any-AI users, $C_c$ Claude users and $M_c$
Claude conversations, with Claude users a subset of any-AI users. Define
$D_c^*=U_c/N_c$, $s_c=C_c/U_c$ and $m_c=M_c/C_c$. For positive counts,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{M_c}{N_c}=D_c^*s_cm_c,\qquad
 AUI_c=K D_c^*s_cm_c,\qquad K=\frac{N_W}{M_W},
 \label{eq:aui_identity}
\end{equation}
where $W$ is the common reference universe used to normalise the index.
Consequently, under aligned definitions,
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_{AUI}=\varepsilon_{D^*}+\varepsilon_s+\varepsilon_m.
 \label{eq:aui_elasticity_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The same additive relation holds for ordinary least-squares log-income slopes
estimated on an identical country sample. The country-invariant normalisation
$K$ affects the intercept rather than the cross-country slope.

A steeper conversation-activity gradient can thus reflect an income gradient
in Claude's share of AI users, in conversations per Claude user, or both.
Neither component is synonymous with paid access: with $\pi_c$ the paid share of Claude users and $m_{F,c},m_{P,c}$ the mean conversation counts of free and paid users, $m_c=(1-\pi_c)m_{F,c}+\pi_cm_{P,c}$, and the index identifies none of these terms. In practice the two sources also do not provide the harmonised counts of equation~\eqref{eq:aui_identity}: Microsoft's estimate incorporates telemetry coverage, scaling and shrinkage, while Anthropic uses sampled conversations, publication thresholds and release-specific product coverage, so the observed gradient difference can reflect measurement and selection as well as behaviour. The empirical analysis accordingly labels its outcomes any-product adoption and per-capita Claude activity, and reads their contrast as consistent with the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} rather than as a test of it. Testing paid upgrading would require country-level user counts by subscription status, usage quantities and effective prices, preferably around a plausibly exogenous change in access conditions.

For Argentina (\$14,650) and T\"urkiye (\$16,300) the 2025 values in the July 2026 WDI update exceed the high-income threshold, although both are classified as upper-middle income.

\paragraph{Local prices.} For each of 218 two-letter storefront codes and each app, I retrieved the App Store product page and extracted the storefront currency and the list of in-app purchases with their displayed prices (the list's title is localised, e.g.\ ``In-App-Käufe''). Plans are identified by name. Where a plan appears with monthly and annual variants, the monthly price is the modal price after discarding variants priced at five or more times the lowest one. The archive retains parsed listing records rather than the original HTML; billing-period inference from names and price magnitudes is a matching heuristic, not independently confirmed SKU metadata. Prices are converted to dollars at market rates of 27 September 2026 from open.er-api.com, which agree with an independent source to within 1.6\%. Requests redirected to the U.S. storefront are coded as no local listing observed (45 codes). Pages returning ``not found'' are coded as unavailable in the audit (ChatGPT: Belarus, China, Hong Kong SAR, Macao SAR, Russia and Venezuela; Claude: the same plus Afghanistan, Myanmar and Yemen; Gemini: Afghanistan, Belarus, China and Russia). App Store prices include value-added or goods-and-services taxes in almost all storefronts and exclude sales taxes in the United States and Canada. They can also exceed web prices because of platform commissions (Claude Max, for example, costs \$124.99 in the U.S. App Store against ``from \$100'' on the web). The audit therefore measures listed prices in one platform-specific payment channel. These are not the vendor's web list prices, and they are not necessarily the amounts charged at checkout, which introductory offers or legacy price points can change.

\paragraph{Diffusion.} The Microsoft AI User Share is the estimated share of people aged 15--64 who used a generative-AI product during the period, derived from anonymised Windows telemetry on visits to 19 AI services, scaled for device and operating-system market shares, mobile use and internet penetration, and shrunk toward the global average for countries whose users mostly opt out of telemetry \citep{misra2025measuring}. Two features of the series affect the analysis. First, economies with insufficient telemetry or populations below two million are pooled into regional aggregates: 36 of the 147 economies share identical series with their neighbours, and they coincide with the economies flagged as ``region-imputed'' in the technical paper. The pooled groups include 20 of the 23 low-income economies in the data (for example, Benin, Ghana, Niger, Nigeria and eight other West African economies all record 8.7\%, 9.3\%, 10.1\% and 10.4\% in the four periods). Country-specific estimates are therefore available for only three low-income economies (The Gambia, Rwanda and Syria). I use country-specific estimates as the main sample and show results with pooled values, and with each pool collapsed to one observation, as robustness checks. Second, Microsoft cautions that several widely used domestic tools, notably in China, are not yet covered \citep{microsoft2026q2}.

The Anthropic AI Usage Index for country $c$ is the country's share of Claude.ai conversations divided by its share of the world's working-age population, from privacy-preserving samples of about one million conversations per window \citep{handa2025which,appel2025uneven,appel2026primitives}. Later releases include consumer Free, Pro and Max accounts without distinguishing their country-level contributions; those account types were already included in the November 2025 sample. The consumer series excludes API and enterprise traffic and is published only for countries meeting release-specific thresholds. I harmonise the index normalisation across releases, without making changing product coverage comparable (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). Google's ATLAS reports a per-capita adoption quintile for Gemini products in April 2026 \citep{iscenko2026atlas}.

\paragraph{Computational workflow.} Generative AI tools, ChatGPT and Codex (OpenAI) and Claude (Anthropic), assisted with code development, extraction review and drafting; the author verified the sources, analysis and text. The replication package (Appendix~\ref{app:data}) contains the frozen inputs, scripts and outputs. Re-running it reproduces every number in the paper, and independent reimplementations of the key regressions in plain NumPy match the reported estimates.

\subsection{Estimands and inference}

For plan $k$, the price regression is $\ln p_{ck}=\alpha_k+\beta_k\ln y_c+u_{ck}$, estimated by ordinary least squares with equal country weights. The coefficient is the cross-country association of listed consumer prices with income within the observed sample; it is not a demand elasticity, and it is not a firm's localisation rule, because taxes, platform commissions and product differences all enter the listing. Because $\ln\AAB_{ck}=\ln(1200)+\ln p_{ck}-\ln y_c$, the burden slope on the same sample is exactly $\beta_k-1$, and a constant reference price imposes a slope of $-1$ by construction; regressing use on that burden would supply no price variation independent of income. The matched Go-to-Plus regression uses the ratio of two listings observed in the same storefront and then compares ratios across economies; it cancels the currency conversion common to both plans but supplies no within-country variation over time.

Usage regressions take the form $\ln U_c=\alpha+\eta\ln y_c+X_c'\gamma+e_c$. Common-sample comparisons use the same countries and income observations for both outcomes. HC3 standard errors describe regression uncertainty conditional on the published indicators; currency-clustered errors are reported for the price regressions, and country-clustered and bootstrap intervals for the difference between usage gradients. None of these incorporates the estimation uncertainty inside the providers' own indicators or selection into publication. Population weighting is reported as a sensitivity because it describes a different estimand.

The audit date, income year, diffusion window and survey year are distinct, and the design neither links contemporaneous price changes to later use nor estimates individual willingness to pay.

The pattern is consistent with the logic of menu design. Localising the \$20 tier invites arbitrage by high-valuation users through foreign accounts, whereas localising a capability-limited entry tier does not, because high-valuation users self-select into the higher tier \citep{mussa1978monopoly}. Where vendors price-discriminate across countries, they sell degraded quality cheaply but barely localise the prices of frontier tiers. By Proposition~\ref{prop:margins}, price should then matter most on the paid margin, which remains unlocalised. Taxes, platform rules and currency schedules also enter the listings, so the slopes are associations in a price menu rather than firms' discrimination parameters, and the audit cannot establish whether a lower-allowance tier suffices for any particular task.

\paragraph{Robustness: currency dependence and a matched-plan comparison.} Prices in the same currency share exchange-rate and platform-pricing shocks, and the currency clusters are highly unbalanced: the Plus sample has 102 USD listings, 25 EUR listings and 37 singleton currencies. Currency-clustered standard errors are accordingly larger than HC3 errors (Table~\ref{tab:price_revision}). For ChatGPT Plus the clustered 95\% interval is $[-0.0002, 0.079]$, so the small positive standard-tier slope is not distinguishable from zero under that dependence assumption, which only strengthens the uniformity finding; the Go slope's clustered interval, $[0.114, 0.173]$, excludes zero. A matched comparison removes currency conversion altogether. Within each storefront, the log Go-to-Plus price ratio has an income slope of 0.104 (currency-clustered s.e.\ 0.007; 164 economies), which on a common sample is exactly the difference between the Go and Plus slopes. Its value is not new information but a check: because the ratio cancels the exchange-rate conversion and any proportional tax common to both plans, the entry-tier gradient is not an artefact of currency conversion. It remains 0.113 within the 102 USD-priced storefronts, 0.112 without them and 0.125 without USD and EUR (Table~\ref{tab:menu_sensitivity} and Figure~\ref{fig:price_ratio}), although no low-income economy remains once USD listings are excluded, so the exclusion samples speak to middle- and high-income markets. Restricting the separate regressions to economies with 2025 income data gives slopes of 0.149 for Go and 0.046 for Plus.

\subsection{Any use: the extensive margin}\label{sec:extensive}

\paragraph{Changes in relative and absolute dispersion.} Between the first half of 2025 and the second quarter of 2026 the median economy's user share rose by 5.5 points in the high-income group, 3.7 in the upper-middle-, 2.0 in the lower-middle- and 0.9 in the low-income group (Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics}). In percentage points the opposite holds: each log point of income is associated with a 1.37-point larger gain (s.e.\ 0.13), the standard deviation rose from 10.9 to 12.8 points, and the gap between the median high- and lower-middle-income economy widened from 16.3 to 20.3 points. This reconciles reports of faster proportional growth in lower-income countries \citep{chatterji2025how} and of a widening divide \citep{microsoft2026h2,liu2025who}, which are two readings of an early-phase diffusion curve, although four vendor-estimated windows cannot establish its shape and measurement error in the initial level also affects growth-on-initial-level regressions.

The measured gradient also steepened, though not continuously. On the 111 economies observed in all five windows, the elasticity was 0.68 in August and November 2025, 0.74 in February 2026, 0.77 in April and 0.73 in May 2026. The increase from August 2025 to February 2026 (0.064, clustered s.e.\ 0.018) is significant, whereas the change from February to May 2026 is not ($-0.006$, s.e.\ 0.016). Anthropic's releases changed over this period, with later releases adding Cowork sessions and monthly windows, so the series describes measured Claude use rather than a strictly like-for-like quantity; estimates on all economies meeting Anthropic's thresholds in each window are similar (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:auitime}), and the steepening is in line with Anthropic's own finding that low-adoption countries fell slightly further behind \citep{massenkoff2026learning}. The composition of use differs as well: the share of Claude conversations devoted to coursework falls by 4.7 points per log point of income, from 35\% in the median low-income economy (five economies) to 17\% in the median high-income economy. This gradient is consistent with evidence that learning is a leading entry point in lower-income regions \citep{microsoft2026q2,daepp2026how} and with Anthropic's observation that coursework may be better suited to free use \citep{appel2026primitives}. A third source, Google's per-capita adoption quintile for Gemini, is also positively but less tightly related to income (Spearman correlation 0.46 across 195 economies), although a rank measure and different product coverage prevent direct elasticity comparison.

\paragraph{Direct estimation of the gradient difference.} On a common sample, the difference of the two log-income slopes equals the slope in a regression of $\ln(AUI_c/D_c)$ on log income, with or without an identical set of controls. The baseline difference is 0.340 (HC3 s.e.\ 0.034); it is 0.356 with 2025 GNI only, 0.266 with region fixed effects and 0.232 with internet, education and urbanisation controls (Table~\ref{tab:gap_revision}). Leaving out each country in turn gives 0.329--0.351, and working-age-population weighting gives 0.476, a different estimand dominated by large countries. None of these calculations separates the vendor-share and conversations-per-user components of equation~\eqref{eq:aui_identity}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the evidence does and does not show}

The evidence supports three statements. Listed prices for the standard tier are nearly identical across countries, and the only material localisation is of a degraded entry tier. A uniform dollar price therefore imposes burdens that differ by a factor of about 45 across income groups, a gradient that survives alternative denominators, benchmarks and income vintages. And the two public usage indicators differ in their income gradients: any use is moderately income-graded, with about half of its gradient coinciding with connectivity and no decline in the ratio of AI users to internet users as the burden of a paid plan rises, whereas per-capita Claude activity is twice as income-graded and became more so between late 2025 and early 2026.

The evidence does not establish that prices cause the steeper Claude gradient. The free-tier model of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts the observed ordering, but so could several alternatives. Language: frontier assistants perform best in high-resource languages, and English-language interactions are over-represented where local languages are poorly supported \citep{daepp2026how}. Product and brand: Claude's share among AI users may vary with awareness, occupation and competing products, and it is less known in many lower-income markets than ChatGPT, Gemini or Meta's assistant. Work composition: Claude's usage skews toward software and professional tasks, whose share of employment rises with income \citep{gmyrek2025generative}. Measurement: Microsoft's estimator shrinks noisy countries toward the global average and floors device-scaling factors, which compresses cross-country variation, while Anthropic's publication thresholds select countries on conversation volume, and the index pools free and paid accounts, so conversations per user may reflect work practices rather than plan status. The robustness checks address some of these concerns, since the gap persists without high-income economies and conditional on education, but the only credible route to a price effect is variation in prices that is not a function of income.

\subsection{A feasible evaluation agenda}

Such variation now exists. ChatGPT Go was rolled out to India and then to 170 further countries on a staggered schedule \citep{openai2026go}; Google introduced and repriced AI Plus and AI Ultra in 2025--2026 \citep{google2026io}; OpenAI changed the allowances and eligibility of its Pro tiers in September 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:tariff}); several markets saw temporary premium tiers bundled with mobile subscriptions; and App Store price schedules change with exchange rates and taxes. Combined with the quarterly Microsoft series and the monthly Anthropic series, these events support difference-in-differences and event-study designs that compare economies before and after a localised tier becomes available.

The requirements are demanding. Outcomes must be measured at the level of the intervention: effective prices, paid conversion, active users, use per user and cancellations for the provider and plan concerned, on stable population bases and product coverage. The timing of tier introductions may respond to market growth and coincide with marketing, bundles or model upgrades, so an event study needs sufficient pre-treatment observations, plausible comparison groups, checks for anticipation and spillovers, and estimators that accommodate heterogeneous effects across rollout cohorts \citep{callaway2021did}. A response of Claude usage to a ChatGPT price change measures a cross-provider effect, not ChatGPT's own-price elasticity of paid adoption, and currency or tax changes also move incomes and other purchases, so they are not automatically valid instruments. Randomised discounts or institutional-access offers, with free-tier substitution and employer-provided access recorded separately, would identify effects on take-up most cleanly. Vendors could make all of these designs far more precise by publishing country-level paid-subscription counts and effective prices.

\subsection{Policy implications}

The results bear on four policy questions, ordered by the margin they concern.

\emph{Connectivity as a precondition, for the extensive margin.} Connection, devices and electricity are preconditions for any use, and half of the use gradient coincides with the connectivity gradient. The flat ratio of AI users to internet users below the high-income group is consistent with the view that access and complements, rather than price, limit whether the connected use AI at all \citep{worldbank2026wdr,itu2025ff}, although it does not demonstrate this, and compute and data infrastructure add a further layer \citep{lehdonvirta2024compute}. Evidence from fast internet in Africa suggests that such investments raise employment and productivity in their own right \citep{hjort2019arrival}.

\emph{Localise frontier access, not only degraded tiers, for the paid margin.} The welfare effects of third-degree price discrimination are ambiguous in general, but they tend to be positive when discrimination opens markets that uniform pricing would leave unserved \citep{varian1985price,aguirre2010monopoly}, as may be the case for frontier tiers whose list price exceeds a quarter of average income. Local-currency storefronts, carrier billing and time- or usage-limited frontier access at locally indexed prices are practical steps; the arbitrage risk that keeps the \$20 tier uniform can be contained with usage caps rather than by withholding frontier models. Whether a lower-allowance tier suffices for a given task is not something the audit can settle.

\emph{Institutional access.} Evidence on AI's productivity effects comes overwhelmingly from employer-provided tools rather than individual subscriptions \citep{brynjolfsson2025generative,cui2024effects}. Schools, universities, public agencies and employers can decouple access from household income; a World Bank trial in Nigeria found sizeable learning gains from a supervised GPT-4 tutoring programme \citep{desimone2025chalkboards}. Research on subsidies for new health products suggests that free trials can raise long-run adoption through learning rather than crowding it out \citep{dupas2014short}, while even small prices can sharply reduce take-up among the poor \citep{cohen2010free}.

\emph{Transparency.} Research on AI access is constrained by the absence of public data on paid subscriptions, effective prices and usage limits by country. Machine-readable disclosure of dated country-level prices, taxes, supported payment methods, plan limits and product definitions, and usage statistics that distinguish free from paid accounts and users from use per user, would allow the designs above to be implemented. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} lists the fields such a record should contain.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

First, burdens are normalised prices rather than expenditures, and quintile incomes are approximations based on survey shares that mix income and consumption concepts; a person living in an economy with a high national-average ratio is not necessarily unable to afford a subscription. Second, the App Store audit observes listed prices in one channel on one day. Listed prices can differ from web prices and from amounts charged at checkout, they include platform commissions and taxes that the regressions do not separate, and the archive stores parsed listing records rather than the original pages, so the monthly billing period of each listing is inferred from names and price magnitudes rather than confirmed from product metadata. Promotions and bundles are not captured. Third, the Microsoft series is a modelled estimate built on desktop telemetry, and country-specific values are unavailable for most low-income economies; results for that group rest on three economies or on regional pools, and the two-indicator comparison includes a single low-income economy. Fourth, the Anthropic index covers one vendor's consumer product and does not distinguish paid from free users, so it proxies the paid margin imperfectly, and its coverage changed across releases. Fifth, all regressions are cross-sectional and descriptive, and their standard errors omit the uncertainty inside the providers' estimators. Sixth, the framework holds preferences, dispersion and task value fixed, omits competing providers, employer access and welfare, and is not calibrated to the usage indicators; the convergence arithmetic uses occupational exposure and population-wide use as proxies for task exposure and within-task adoption.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Generative AI is priced as a global product and experienced as a local one. The standard \$20 plan is listed at almost the same price everywhere, so it costs less than 1\% of average income in the median rich country and more than a quarter in the median poor one, and it fails the broadband affordability benchmark for most of the world's working-age population and, in low-income economies, even for the richest fifth. Only OpenAI's entry tier is partly localised, and its localisation reaches about 60\% of what indexing to local price levels would imply.

The public usage evidence is consistent with free tiers having taken the price out of the decision whether to use AI at all: on that margin, about half of the income gradient coincides with connectivity, and the ratio of AI users to internet users does not fall as the burden of a paid plan rises. How much frontier capability people use, by contrast, remains tied to income; per-capita Claude activity has a gradient twice as steep, and it steepened between August 2025 and February 2026. Cross-country data cannot say whether price is what ties frontier use to income rather than language, product reach or the composition of work. The staggered rollout of localised tiers and the recent changes in allowances now make that question answerable, provided that prices, entitlements and paid use are recorded at the level at which they change.

The conditional evidence that AI helps less-experienced workers most is real, but conditional equalisation does not aggregate into convergence unless access and exposure keep pace, and on current numbers they do not. Whether they will depends more on who can access or afford frontier models, for how much use and how soon after release, than on how capable the models become.

\section{A conditional framework for free and paid access}\label{app:tiermodel}
\label{sec:conditional_access}

Consider a connected individual choosing between no use, a free tier and a paid
tier. Let $v>0$ denote the individual's monthly monetary valuation of one unit
of effective AI service. For example, $v=\theta w$, where $w$ is monthly income
and $\theta$ captures the relevance of AI to the individual's activities. Let
$0<q_F<q_P$ describe the tiers' service levels, $p_c>0$ the monthly paid price,
and $\tau>0$ a monthly monetary equivalent of the time, learning and other
participation costs common to both tiers. Utilities are
\begin{equation}
 u_0=0,\qquad u_F=vq_F-\tau,\qquad u_P=vq_P-\tau-p_c.
 \label{eq:tier_utilities}
\end{equation}
These service levels are theoretical objects; they need not correspond to
particular models, observed subscription labels or a measured technological
frontier. The framework omits competing suppliers, employer-provided access,
sharing and usage-dependent participation costs.

Write $\Delta q=q_P-q_F$. Direct comparison of all three options gives the
thresholds for any use and paid use:
\begin{equation}
 T_{D,c}=\min\left\{\frac{\tau}{q_F},\frac{\tau+p_c}{q_P}\right\},
 \qquad
 T_{P,c}=\max\left\{\frac{p_c}{\Delta q},\frac{\tau+p_c}{q_P}\right\}.
 \label{eq:general_thresholds}
\end{equation}
Thus a free option does not, by itself, make aggregate adoption independent of
the paid price. A sufficiently attractive paid option can draw individuals
directly from non-use.

Let $\kappa_c$ be the population share with the connectivity and complementary
resources needed to participate. Conditional on belonging to this group, assume
\begin{equation}
 \ln v\sim N(\mu_c,s^2),\qquad
 \mu_c=\mu_0+\ln(y_c/y_0),\qquad s>0,
 \label{eq:conditional_distribution}
\end{equation}
where $y_c$ is a country income index and $y_0$ a reference value. This
location-shift assumption is a stylisation, not an identity linking national
GNI to household incomes or returns to AI. Defining the distribution conditional
on eligibility makes the multiplication by $\kappa_c$ explicit; applying the
same distribution unconditionally would require an additional selection
assumption. Holding qualities, participation costs and dispersion common,
the population shares using any tier and the paid tier are
\begin{equation}
 D_c=\kappa_c\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_c-\ln T_{D,c}}{s}\right),\qquad
 P_c=\kappa_c\Phi\left(\frac{\mu_c-\ln T_{P,c}}{s}\right).
 \label{eq:general_shares}
\end{equation}
The assumption of a common $\tau$ is substantive. If participation costs vary as $\tau_c\propto y_c^\lambda$, the nested-regime adoption slope becomes $\varepsilon_\kappa+(1-\lambda)h(z_D)/s$ even at a fixed free service level. Monetary time costs can rise with wages; the baseline assumption therefore rules out an economically relevant source of slope variation. Arbitrary heterogeneity in
participation costs cannot generally be absorbed into $v$ while retaining both
threshold formulas and a common paid price.

\paragraph{Threshold ordering.}
If
\begin{equation}
 \frac{p_c}{\Delta q}>\frac{\tau}{q_F},
 \label{eq:nested_condition}
\end{equation}
then $T_{D,c}=\tau/q_F$ and $T_{P,c}=p_c/\Delta q$.
Within this regime, a marginal paid-price change leaves $D_c$ unchanged,
provided $\kappa_c$, qualities and participation costs do not change. If the
inequality is reversed, both thresholds equal $(\tau+p_c)/q_P$ and paid price
affects any use as well as paid use.

\paragraph{Proof.}
The free tier is preferable to non-use when $v\geq\tau/q_F$. The paid tier is
preferable to non-use when $v\geq(\tau+p_c)/q_P$, and preferable to the free tier
when $v\geq p_c/\Delta q$. These comparisons establish
equation~\eqref{eq:general_thresholds}. Under
condition~\eqref{eq:nested_condition}, the three thresholds are ordered as
$\tau/q_F<(\tau+p_c)/q_P<p_c/\Delta q$. Reversing the condition reverses this
ordering, establishing both regimes. At equality the thresholds coincide.
\qed

\begin{proposition}[Local income gradients]\label{prop:margins}
Within the strict nested-threshold regime, define
$z_D=(\mu_c-\ln(\tau/q_F))/s$,
$z_P=(\mu_c-\ln(p_c/\Delta q))/s$,
$h(z)=\phi(z)/\Phi(z)$,
$\beta=\partial\ln p_c/\partial\ln y_c$, and
$\varepsilon_\kappa=\partial\ln\kappa_c/\partial\ln y_c$.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_D=\varepsilon_\kappa+\frac{h(z_D)}{s},\qquad
 \varepsilon_P=\varepsilon_\kappa+(1-\beta)\frac{h(z_P)}{s}.
 \label{eq:income_gradients}
\end{equation}
Uniform pricing gives $\varepsilon_P>\varepsilon_D$. Holding the local price
level and hence the threshold values fixed, this ordering persists if
$\beta<\beta^*\equiv1-h(z_D)/h(z_P)$, where $0<\beta^*<1$.\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Proof.}
Differentiating the logarithms of equation~\eqref{eq:general_shares} yields
equation~\eqref{eq:income_gradients}, since
$\partial\mu_c/\partial\ln y_c=1$. The strict threshold ordering implies
$z_P<z_D$. The lower-tail inverse Mills ratio $h$ is strictly decreasing, so
$h(z_P)>h(z_D)$. Subtracting the elasticities and solving for their equality
gives $\beta^*$. \qed

The comparison across $\beta$ is local. One implementation compares schedules
$p_\beta(y)=p_0(y/y_0)^\beta$ at their common anchor $y_0$. Away from that anchor,
changing the pricing schedule also moves the paid threshold; the proposition
does not establish a global effect of a localisation policy. Nor does it
establish welfare effects. Its substantive implication is conditional: with
nested thresholds and the maintained distributional assumptions, a free tier
can separate the determinants of initial adoption from those of upgrading.

In the reversed-threshold regime, the same calculation instead gives
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_D=\varepsilon_P
 =\varepsilon_\kappa+
 \left(1-\beta\frac{p_c}{\tau+p_c}\right)\frac{h(z)}{s},
 \qquad z=\frac{\mu_c-\ln((\tau+p_c)/q_P)}{s}.
 \label{eq:reversed_gradients}
\end{equation}
There is then no interval in which the free tier is the individual's optimal
choice. This limiting case makes clear why the empirical presence of free
products alone cannot establish price independence. More generally, differences
across countries in language support, time costs, service quality, selection
into connectivity or the dispersion of valuations introduce additional terms
into the gradients. These possibilities are substantively relevant and are not
ruled out by an income regression. The framework should therefore organise
questions about access while leaving these competing explanations open.

\section{A \$500 reference-fee scenario}\label{app:500}

\section{A protocol for recording access conditions}\label{app:protocol}

A recurring fee does not determine the cost of completing useful work. A reproducible observation of access conditions should record the country, observation time, billing channel, provider, plan and billing period; the original currency and tax convention; listing and checkout status; subscriber cohort; included allowance and reset rules; model and tool eligibility; and overage terms. Missing terms should remain missing rather than be assigned a provider-wide constant. The replication package supplies a field dictionary for these observations (\nolinkurl{publication_upgrade/access_measurement_fields.csv}). It is a proposed extension of the present audit, not a newly collected dataset.

For a prespecified workload $B$, quality rule $q$ and deadline $d$, define the feasible set $\mathcal F(B,q,d;c,t)$ of service configurations that actually meet all three requirements in country $c$ at time $t$. The minimum cash expenditure is
\[
C(B,q,d;c,t)=\inf_{a\in\mathcal F(B,q,d;c,t)}\operatorname{cashcost}(a).
\]
The comparison must state whether free, API, institutional and local-model configurations are included and how fixed hardware or subscription costs are allocated. Feasibility requires task-level observations of correctness, language, model version, wall-clock completion time, retries, billed use and failures. This paper does not observe those quantities and does not estimate $C$. A prospective evaluation should report total expenditure and success rates alongside cost per successful task, since excluding failed attempts would understate costs. The protocol distinguishes measured subscription commitment from a future quality- and deadline-constrained access outcome.
